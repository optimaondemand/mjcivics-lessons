📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do regimes collapse, and can a constitution be designed to resist collapse?
This module opened in Week 1 with Aristotle's account of Cleisthenes rebuilding Athenian citizenship from the ground up. It closes with Aristotle again, this time writing not as a historian of one city's reforms but as a systematic theorist of political stability in his Politics, Books IV and V. Having catalogued and compared the constitutions of dozens of Greek city-states, Aristotle turned to a question every framer in Philadelphia was implicitly wrestling with.

📚 PART 1 · TWO WAYS A REGIME CAN COLLAPSE

Aristotle's answer in Book V centers on inequality and the perception of it, not necessarily inequality of wealth alone, but any group's sense that it is not receiving its rightful share of honor, office, or power relative to its actual contribution or numbers. Democracies fall, he argued, when the poor majority feels excluded from real influence; oligarchies fall when a wealthy few grow so exclusive that everyone else unites against them. Revolutions, in Aristotle's analysis, are rarely random explosions of unrest; they follow a discoverable logic rooted in exactly which group feels shortchanged by the current arrangement of power.

According to Aristotle, what single underlying factor drives most revolutions, more than wealth inequality by itself?
The perception of not receiving one's rightful share of honor, office, or power relative to one's contribution or numbers, and the intemperance of demagogues who stir that feeling up.
📚 PART 2 · HAMILTON'S DESIGN AS A PRACTICAL ANSWER

Read against this backdrop, Hamilton's careful electoral design in Federalist No. 68 looks like a direct, practical application of Aristotle's diagnosis. An executive chosen entirely by a wealthy elite, cut off from any "sense of the people," would risk exactly the resentment Aristotle warned could topple an oligarchy. An executive chosen by unchecked mass passion, with no filter for qualification, risked the opposite failure mode Aristotle also catalogued: a democracy degenerating into instability once it can no longer distinguish competent leadership from popular flattery. Nearly twenty-two centuries separate Aristotle's Athens from Hamilton's Philadelphia, but the same underlying question, who gets to rule, and will everyone else accept it as legitimate, runs unbroken between them, closing the circle this module opened.
