📚 PART 1 · TWO WAYS A REGIME CAN COLLAPSE4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 8.1. This week's reading introduces no new Subject Vocabulary terms.

demagogue (DEM-uh-gog)
a leader who wins power by appealing to emotion and prejudice rather than reasoned argument.
electors (ee-LEK-terz)
specially chosen people who meet once, in separate state groups, to formally select the president.

This time, he is writing not as a historian of one city's reforms, but as a systematic theorist of political stability, in his book Politics, Books IV and V. Aristotle had catalogued and compared the constitutions of dozens of Greek city-states. From that huge body of comparison, he turned to a question every framer in Philadelphia was implicitly wrestling with: why do regimes collapse, and can a constitution be designed to resist collapse?

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary
Aristotle (AIR-ih-stot-uhl)
a Greek philosopher who catalogued and compared the constitutions of dozens of Greek city-states to study why regimes fall apart.

Aristotle's answer in Book V centers on inequality, and especially the perception of inequality. This is not necessarily inequality of wealth alone. It can be any group's sense that it is not receiving its rightful share of honor, office, or power relative to its actual contribution or numbers. Democracies fall, Aristotle argued, when the poor majority feels excluded from real influence. Oligarchies fall when a wealthy few grow so exclusive that everyone else unites against them. Revolutions, in Aristotle's analysis, are rarely random explosions of unrest. They follow a discoverable logic, rooted in exactly which group feels shortchanged by the current arrangement of power.

⚡ FLASHCARD · CLICK TO FLIP
Test yourself on Aristotle's diagnosis before you read his own words on the next page.
According to Aristotle, what single underlying factor drives most revolutions, more than wealth inequality by itself?
The perception of not receiving one's rightful share of honor, office, or power relative to one's contribution or numbers, and the intemperance of demagogues who stir that feeling up.

Aristotle offered one further piece of advice worth carrying forward. In Book IV, he argued that the most stable regimes are usually the ones with a large, secure middle class, citizens who are neither desperately poor nor extravagantly rich. A large middle class, he reasoned, has the most to lose from instability and the least reason to envy or resent either the very poor or the very wealthy. Recall Solon's zeugetai from Week 1, the middle-class farmers whose status Pisistratus deliberately protected even after seizing power. Aristotle's theory helps explain why: a broad, secure middle class has been recognized, since ancient Athens, as one of the most reliable foundations a lasting republic can have.

📚 PART 2 · HAMILTON'S DESIGN AS A PRACTICAL ANSWER6 min

Aristotle's method itself is worth noticing. Rather than reasoning from pure abstract principle alone, as some philosophers do, Aristotle and his students reportedly collected and compared the actual written constitutions of over one hundred fifty Greek city-states before he wrote his Politics. His conclusions about why regimes fall are built on this unusually wide base of real comparative evidence, not on a single city's experience or a single thinker's intuition. This same comparative habit, studying many real examples before drawing a general conclusion, is exactly what Madison was doing in his own “Ancient and Modern Confederacies” memo back in Week 6.

Notice, too, how directly this week's primary source excerpt connects to Hamilton's own worry. Aristotle writes that revolutions in democracies are “generally caused by the intemperance of demagogues(DEM-uh-gog)a leader who wins power by appealing to emotion and prejudice rather than reasoned argument.,” who either personally attack wealthy citizens or stir up the people against them. This is precisely the danger Hamilton names when he warns that direct election could reward a leader who wins support through emotional appeal rather than genuine merit. Two writers, separated by more than two thousand years and writing in entirely different languages and political systems, converge on the same specific warning about the same specific kind of leader.

This lesson closes Module 2 as a whole. Over the past six weeks, you have moved from Rome's mixed constitution, through Hobbes and Locke's competing accounts of human nature, through Montesquieu's separated powers and Rousseau's general will, through the actual Convention that built a government out of all these ideas, and finally through the Federalist Papers that defended it clause by clause. Module 3 now turns from these founding arguments to the government those arguments actually produced: the specific structure and powers of Congress, the presidency, and the federal courts. Keep Aristotle's question in mind as you go: every structural detail you are about to study is, at bottom, one more attempt to answer who should rule, and why everyone else should accept it.

Read against this backdrop, Hamilton's careful electoral design in Federalist No. 68 looks like a direct, practical application of Aristotle's diagnosis. An executive chosen entirely by a wealthy elite, cut off from any “sense of the people,” would risk exactly the resentment Aristotle warned could topple an oligarchy. An executive chosen by unchecked mass passion, with no filter for qualification, risked the opposite failure mode Aristotle also catalogued. That failure mode is a democracy degenerating into instability once it can no longer distinguish competent leadership from popular flattery. Nearly twenty-two centuries separate Aristotle's Athens from Hamilton's Philadelphia. But the same underlying question, who gets to rule, and will everyone else accept it as legitimate, runs unbroken between them, closing the circle this module opened.
