Hamilton and the Problem of Choosing an Executive
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single leader looks like a king. A legislature with no strong executive just failed under the Articles. How do you choose the person who has to walk that line?
Of the three branches the Convention created, none worried the delegates more than the executive. A single national leader looked dangerously close to the king they had just fought a revolution to escape, but a legislature with no strong executive to check it had just failed catastrophically under the Articles of Confederation. Alexander Hamilton, the most forceful defender of a strong presidency among the framers, took up the specific question of how to choose that executive in Federalist No. 68.

📚 PART 1 · TWO REJECTED METHODS

Hamilton considered the two obvious methods and rejected both. Direct popular election, he worried, risked exactly the kind of unchecked mass enthusiasm you studied in the Athenian Assembly's vote for the Sicilian Expedition last week: a charismatic demagogue could sweep an emotional majority without possessing the requisite qualifications for the office. Selection by the legislature, meanwhile, risked the opposite danger: an executive who owed his position entirely to Congress would have every incentive to become its servant rather than an independent check on it, undermining the separation of powers from Week 4 and Week 7 before it even began operating.

📚 PART 2 · A COMPROMISE DESIGNED AGAINST BOTH RISKS

Hamilton's compromise, and the Convention's, was a body of specially chosen electors, meeting only once, in separate state groups, with no ongoing existence as an institution, making them, in Hamilton's view, resistant both to mob passion and to manipulation or bribery, since there was no standing body to corrupt over time.

Notice the pattern across every branch you have studied this module: not eliminate risk, but design specific structures, a Senate, a Preamble's stated purposes, an electoral process, to manage risks the framers believed were permanent features of political life, not problems that could ever be solved once and for all.
