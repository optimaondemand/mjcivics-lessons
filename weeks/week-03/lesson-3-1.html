John Locke and the Right to Undo a Government

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a government breaks the people's trust, does it matter whether that government is a king, a council, or an elected majority?
Rome's mixed constitution held together for centuries, but it was not built on a theory of individual rights. A Roman citizen's protections came from his place within the system, not from any claim about what he was owed simply by being human. Seventeenth-century England produced a different kind of argument, one that would eventually reach Philadelphia: the claim that certain rights belong to a person before any government exists at all, and that government itself is only legitimate insofar as it protects those rights.

📚 PART 1 · THE STATE OF NATURE AND NATURAL RIGHTS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Locke says people leave the state of nature because enforcing their own rights alone is "inconvenient and unsafe." Before reading further: does that sound more like a practical bargain, or a moral rule everyone must obey no matter what? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Rights Before Government

The philosopher who made this case most influentially was John Locke, writing in the years around England's Glorious Revolution of 1688. Locke argued that in a state of nature, a condition before or outside organized government, people already possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property, discoverable through reason rather than granted by any king or parliament. Because enforcing those rights alone is inconvenient and unsafe (a lone individual has no police, no courts, no army), people agree to leave the state of nature and form a political community. This agreement is the social contract: individuals consent to be governed, and in exchange, government's entire job is to secure the rights they already had.

📚 PART 2 · A TRUST THAT ANY GOVERNMENT CAN BREAK

No Government Is Exempt

Notice what follows from this, and notice that it follows no matter who is doing the governing. If government exists only to protect pre-existing rights, then government is not the source of those rights and cannot justly take them away without cause, whether that government is a king, a council of aristocrats, a small ruling oligarchy, or an elected democratic majority. Locke pushed this reasoning to a conclusion few rulers of any kind wanted to hear: when a government persistently violates the trust placed in it, when rulers act as though the people exist for them, rather than the reverse, the people are entitled to resist and, if necessary, replace that government. The form of government makes no difference to the argument; only whether it keeps or breaks the people's trust does. This week's Locke reading, from late in his Second Treatise of Government, spells out exactly when and why a government dissolves: not simply when a ruler is unpopular, but when the fundamental trust between ruler and ruled has been broken.

📚 PART 3 · FROM ENGLAND TO PHILADELPHIA

This idea will not stay confined to England, and it will not stay confined to arguments against kings specifically. When the American colonists broke with Britain a century later, their central conflict was actually with Parliament, not the King himself, and in 1775 the Continental Congress's Olive Branch Petition appealed directly to King George III to intervene against Parliament on the colonists' behalf: an implicit appeal to the very reasoning you are studying this week, that even a legislature can breach the people's trust and forfeit its claim to obedience. When you reach Week 5's study of the Declaration of Independence, you will recognize this week's vocabulary almost word for word, because Thomas Jefferson had Locke's Second Treatise close at hand when he wrote it.
