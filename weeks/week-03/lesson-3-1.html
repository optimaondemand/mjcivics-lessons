📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a government breaks the people's trust, does it matter whether that government is a king, a council, or an elected majority?
Rome's mixed constitution held together for centuries. But it was not built on a theory of individual rights. A Roman citizen's protections came from his place within the system. They did not come from any claim about what he was owed simply for being human, whether he was rich or poor, a patrician or a plebeian.

dissolve / dissolution (dih-ZOLV / dis-uh-LOO-shun)

natural rights (NACH-er-ul RYTS)

social contract (SOH-shul KON-trakt)
the agreement people make to leave the state of nature and form a government, trading some independence for protection of their rights.
state of nature
a condition before or outside any organized government, where people are free but have no police, courts, or army to protect their rights.
Tyranny (TEER-uh-nee)
monarchy corrupted so a one-man ruler serves only his own private gain.
Monarchy (MON-ar-kee)
kingship used for the common good of everyone, in Polybius's framework.

Seventeenth-century England produced a very different kind of argument. This argument would eventually reach Philadelphia, where the American founders wrote the Constitution more than a hundred years later. The claim was this: certain rights belong to a person before any government exists at all. Government itself is only legitimate, or rightful, if it protects those rights. If a government fails to protect them, or actively attacks them, something has gone seriously wrong.

The philosopher who made this case most famously was John Locke. He wrote in the years around England's Glorious Revolution of 1688, when the English forced out one king and replaced him with another under new limits set by Parliament. Locke argued that in a state of naturea condition before or outside any organized government, where people are free but have no police, courts, or army to protect their rights., a condition before or outside any organized government, people already have natural rightsNACH-er-ul RYTSrights people have simply by being human, before any government exists: life, liberty, and property, according to Locke.. These rights are life, liberty, and property. People do not need a king or a parliament to grant them these rights. Instead, people can discover that they have these rights just by using reason and thinking clearly about what it means to be human. Imagine, for a moment, a person living completely alone, with no government of any kind nearby. That person still owns his own life. He is still free to make his own choices. He can still claim the fruits of his own labor as his property. Government did not give him any of that. But there is a serious problem with the state of nature. If a person's rights are violated there, he has no police to call, no court to bring his case to, and no army to protect him. Enforcing your own rights all by yourself is both inconvenient and unsafe. Because of this danger, Locke argued, people agree to leave the state of nature. They form a political community together instead. This agreement is called the social contractSOH-shul KON-traktthe agreement people make to leave the state of nature and form a government, trading some independence for protection of their rights.. Individuals agree to be governed. In exchange, the government's entire job is to protect the rights they already had before it existed. A government under the social contract does not hand out rights like gifts. It exists because people already agreed that protecting life, liberty, and property was worth giving up some of their independence for.

📚 PART 2 · A TRUST THAT ANY GOVERNMENT CAN BREAK4 min

Notice what follows from this idea, and notice that it follows no matter who is doing the governing. If government exists only to protect rights that already existed, then government did not create those rights in the first place. This means government cannot justly take those rights away without a very good reason. This is true whether the government is a king, a council of aristocrats, a small ruling group, or an elected democratic majority. Locke pushed this reasoning to a conclusion that few rulers of any kind wanted to hear. When a government keeps breaking the trust the people placed in it, when rulers start acting as though the people exist to serve them, instead of the other way around, the people are allowed to resist. If necessary, they are allowed to replace that government entirely. The type of government makes no difference to this argument. What matters is only whether the government keeps the people's trust or breaks it. Locke is drawing a careful, sharp line here. A ruler who simply makes decisions people dislike has not broken the social contract. A ruler who tries to seize people's property without their consent, or who rules by raw force instead of settled law, has crossed that line completely. This week's Locke reading comes from late in his book, the Second Treatise of Government. It spells out exactly when and why a government dissolvesdih-ZOLV / dis-uh-LOO-shunwhen a government falls apart because it has broken the people's trust, not simply because a ruler is unpopular., or falls apart. A government does not dissolve simply because a ruler becomes unpopular. It dissolves when the basic trust between ruler and ruled has truly been broken.

📚 PART 3 · FROM ENGLAND TO PHILADELPHIA4 min

This idea did not stay confined to England. It also did not stay confined to arguments against kings specifically. Locke's ideas crossed the Atlantic Ocean and took root among American colonists, many of whom read his works widely in the decades before the Revolution. A century after Locke wrote, the American colonists broke with Britain. Their central conflict was actually with Parliament, the British lawmaking body, not with the King himself. In 1775, the Continental Congress sent the Olive Branch Petition. This petition appealed directly to King George III, asking him to step in against Parliament on the colonists' behalf. This was an unspoken appeal to the very reasoning you are studying this week: that even a legislature, or lawmaking body, can break the people's trust and lose its claim to their obedience. When you reach Week 5's study of the Declaration of Independence, you will recognize this week's vocabulary almost word for word. That is because Thomas Jefferson had Locke's Second Treatise close at hand when he wrote it.
