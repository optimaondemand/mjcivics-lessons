📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If people are naturally free and roughly equal, does that make peace easier to reach, or harder?
Locke was not the first Englishman to reason from a "state of nature" to a theory of government. A generation earlier, having lived through the chaos of the English Civil War, Thomas Hobbes reached a starkly different conclusion using strikingly similar tools. Hobbes agreed with Locke that people are naturally free and roughly equal in their capacities. But where Locke saw the state of nature as merely inconvenient, Hobbes saw it as catastrophic.

Why Equality Leads to Conflict

Hobbes's reasoning ran like this: if people are equal in ability and each is free to pursue his own preservation and advantage, then any two people can want the same scarce thing, land, safety, glory, and neither has a natural right to override the other. With no common power to judge between them, conflict is not an occasional risk; it is the default condition. Hobbes called this condition, without any government to restrain it, a "war of every man against every man," and famously described the resulting life as "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."

📚 PART 2 · WHY A PROMISE ALONE MEANS NOTHING

This is also why Hobbes insisted that promises and covenants, on their own, could never be binding in the state of nature. A promise is only as good as some power capable of punishing whoever breaks it, and in a state of nature no such power exists. Worse, if I plan to keep my word while I have no way of knowing whether you will keep yours, I gain nothing and risk everything by going first, so even a sincere person has every reason to break a promise the moment it stops serving him, and every reason to expect the same from everyone else. Hobbes put this bluntly: covenants without the sword to enforce them are, in his words, "but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all." This is precisely the problem his solution is built to solve.

📚 PART 3 · THE LEVIATHAN SOLUTION

Hobbes's solution was as bold as his diagnosis was bleak. To escape this war, he argued, people must surrender their individual right to judge and enforce their own claims entirely, transferring it to a single sovereign power, what he called the Leviathan, after the biblical sea monster, a figure representing an authority so overwhelming that no one would dare challenge it. Only a power that strong, Hobbes believed, could finally make promises mean something, since it alone could punish whoever broke one. Crucially, for Hobbes, this transfer had to be close to total and, once made, essentially irrevocable: a sovereign with divided or limited power, he worried, would not be strong enough to prevent society from sliding back into war. This is the sharpest point of contrast with Locke, who believed rights survived the formation of government and could justify resistance. Hobbes believed almost any government, however severe, was preferable to no government at all. As you read this week's second primary source, ask yourself which philosopher's diagnosis of human nature you find more convincing, and notice that the American founders, as you will see across the rest of this module, sided far more with Locke than with Hobbes, while still taking Hobbes's warning about disorder seriously.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Locke and Hobbes both start from a state of nature and both reason their way to a theory of legitimate government, yet they land in almost opposite places: Locke trusts people to keep their rights even under government, while Hobbes trusts almost nothing except an overwhelming sovereign.
