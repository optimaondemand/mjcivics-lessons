📚 PART 1 · SAME STARTING POINT, DARKER CONCLUSION6 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing four terms from Lesson 3.1, plus one new term for Hobbes's argument.

dissolve / dissolution (dih-ZOLV / dis-uh-LOO-shun)
when a government falls apart because it has broken the people's trust, not simply because a ruler is unpopular.
Leviathan (luh-VY-uh-thun)
a giant sea monster described in the Bible; Hobbes's name for the single, overwhelming ruler he believed government needed.
natural rights (NACH-er-ul RYTS)
rights people have simply by being human, before any government exists: life, liberty, and property, according to Locke.
social contract (SOH-shul KON-trakt)
the agreement people make to leave the state of nature and form a government, trading some independence for protection of their rights.
state of nature
a condition before or outside any organized government, where people are free but have no police, courts, or army to protect their rights.

Locke was not the first Englishman to reason from a “state of naturea condition before or outside any organized government, where people are free but have no police, courts, or army to protect their rights.” toward a theory of government. A generation earlier, Thomas Hobbes reached a very different conclusion using strikingly similar tools. Hobbes had lived through the chaos of the English Civil War, a brutal conflict in the 1640s that tore England apart and ended with the country's own king executed by his own Parliament. That experience shaped everything Hobbes later wrote about government. Hobbes agreed with Locke about one important thing: people are naturally free, and roughly equal in their abilities. A strong man can be killed in his sleep by a weaker one, so even the strongest person is never fully safe from everyone else. But where Locke saw the state of nature as merely inconvenient, something people would eventually want to leave for practical reasons, Hobbes saw it as an outright disaster, a condition no reasonable person could tolerate for long.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Hobbes says that without government, life would be “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.” Do you find that more convincing, or less convincing, than Locke's milder description of the state of nature as merely “inconvenient”? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Hobbes's reasoning went like this. If people are equal in ability, and each person is free to pursue his own safety and advantage, then any two people can end up wanting the same scarce thing. That thing might be land, food, safety, or glory. Neither person has any natural right to take it over the other. With no common power to judge between them and settle the dispute, conflict is not just an occasional risk. It is the normal condition of life. Hobbes called this condition, with no government to hold it back, a “war of every man against every man.” Notice that Hobbes did not mean constant fighting every single moment. He meant a constant readiness for fighting, a world where a person could never fully relax his guard. Hobbes named three main reasons people quarrel in this condition: competition over scarce resources, distrust of what other people might do first, and the simple desire for glory and reputation. All three reasons exist whether or not anyone is actually poor or actually threatened, which is part of why Hobbes thought the danger was so hard to escape. He famously described the resulting life as “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.”

📚 PART 2 · WHY A PROMISE ALONE MEANS NOTHING4 min

This is also why Hobbes insisted that promises and agreements, on their own, could never truly hold up in the state of nature. A promise is only as good as some power that can punish whoever breaks it. In a state of nature, no such power exists at all. There is an even worse problem hiding underneath this one. Suppose I plan to keep my word, but I have no way of knowing whether you will keep yours. If I go first and follow through, I gain nothing if you break your word, and I risk everything. So even a completely sincere person has every reason to break a promise the moment it stops helping him. And that same sincere person has every reason to expect the exact same behavior from everyone else around him. Hobbes put this bluntly. He said agreements without a sword to enforce them are, in his own words, “but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all.” This is exactly the problem his solution was built to solve.

📚 PART 3 · THE LEVIATHAN SOLUTION4 min

Hobbes's solution was as bold as his diagnosis was bleak. To escape this war, he argued, people must give up their individual right to judge and enforce their own claims entirely. Instead, they must hand that power over to a single, overwhelming ruler. Hobbes called this ruler the Leviathanluh-VY-uh-thuna giant sea monster described in the Bible; Hobbes's name for the single, overwhelming ruler he believed government needed., after a giant sea monster described in the Bible. The name stood for an authority so powerful that no one would dare challenge it. Only a power that strong, Hobbes believed, could finally make promises mean something, since only it could punish anyone who broke one. For Hobbes, this handover of power had to be almost total, covering nearly every part of a person's public life. Once made, it could not really be taken back by the people who made it. He worried that a ruler with divided or limited power would not be strong enough to stop society from sliding back into war the moment real trouble arrived. This is the sharpest difference between Hobbes and Locke. Locke believed rights survived even after government was formed, and that those rights could justify resisting a government that broke its trust. Hobbes believed that almost any government, however harsh or demanding, was better than no government at all. As you read this week's second primary source, ask yourself which philosopher's view of human nature you find more convincing. Notice, too, that the American founders, as you will see across the rest of this module, sided far more with Locke than with Hobbes, even while they still took Hobbes's warning about disorder and chaos seriously. Both philosophers agreed that people need government. They disagreed sharply about how much freedom people could safely keep once that government existed.
