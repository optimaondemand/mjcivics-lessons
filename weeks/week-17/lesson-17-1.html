📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How did the United States decide to respond to Soviet expansion after 1945, and why did a single speech need an alliance behind it?
Foreign policy is what a nation does beyond its own borders; domestic policy is what it does within them. The two are rarely as separate as that definition suggests: a decision to fight a war abroad reshapes taxes, the draft, and public opinion at home, as Week 16 just showed. This week examines how the United States has organized its relationships with the rest of the world since 1945, starting with a doctrine that shaped nearly fifty years of American foreign policy.

📚 PART 1 · THE TRUMAN DOCTRINE

In March 1947, with Soviet-backed pressure threatening Greece and Turkey, President Harry Truman asked Congress for aid to those countries, framing the request in far broader terms than a single regional crisis. The Truman Doctrine committed the United States to supporting "free peoples" resisting subjugation "by armed minorities or by outside pressures" anywhere in the world. It was the founding statement of the policy historians call containment: not rolling back Soviet communism by force, but preventing its further spread.

⚡ FLIP THE CARD
What is "containment"?
Truman's 1947 policy of preventing the further spread of Soviet communism, without necessarily reversing it where it already existed. Distinct from "rolling back" communism by force.
⚡ FLIP THE CARD
What is NATO's core promise?
Founded in 1949 by the United States and eleven other nations: an attack on any member is treated as an attack on all.
📚 PART 2 · AN ALLIANCE TO MAKE CONTAINMENT CREDIBLE

Containment needed institutions to make it credible over the long run, not just a single speech. In 1949, the United States and eleven other nations formed the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), a mutual-defense alliance built on the promise that an attack on any member would be treated as an attack on all. NATO gave containment teeth: any Soviet move against Western Europe would now risk war with the combined military power of the whole alliance, not just the country directly attacked.
