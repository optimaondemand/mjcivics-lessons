📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How did the United States decide to respond to Soviet expansion after 1945, and why did a single speech need an alliance behind it?
Foreign policy is what a nation does beyond its own borders; domestic policy is what it does within them. The two are rarely as separate as that definition suggests: a decision to fight a war abroad reshapes taxes, the draft, and public opinion at home, as Week 16 just showed. This week examines how the United States has organized its relationships with the rest of the world since 1945, starting with a doctrine that shaped nearly fifty years of American foreign policy.

In March 1947, with Soviet-backed pressure threatening Greece and Turkey, President Harry Truman asked Congress for aid to those countries, framing the request in far broader terms than a single regional crisis. The Truman Doctrinethe 1947 commitment to support “free peoples” resisting subjugation anywhere in the world; the founding statement of containment. committed the United States to supporting "free peoples" resisting subjugation "by armed minorities or by outside pressures" anywhere in the world. It was the founding statement of the policy historians call containmentkun-TAYN-mentthe Cold War policy of preventing the further spread of Soviet communism, without necessarily rolling it back where it already existed.: not rolling back Soviet communism by force, but preventing its further spread.

Truman's request was as much about Britain as it was about Greece and Turkey. Britain had been propping up the Greek government in its civil war against communist-backed insurgents, but by early 1947 Britain's own postwar economy could no longer afford the commitment, and British officials told Washington directly that the United States would have to take over the burden or watch both countries fall. Truman's speech was the American answer to that handoff, and it committed the United States to a role Britain had held for over a century: guarantor of a favorable balance of power well beyond its own borders.

📚 PART 2 · AN ALLIANCE TO MAKE CONTAINMENT CREDIBLE7 min

Containment's economic companion arrived the following year. In 1948, Secretary of State George Marshall proposed a massive program of American economic aid to help rebuild war-shattered European economies, soon known as the Marshall Planthe massive American economic aid program to rebuild war-shattered European economies, as a form of containment.. The reasoning was straightforward: desperate, impoverished populations were more vulnerable to communist movements promising radical change, so rebuilding prosperity was itself a form of containment. The United States ultimately provided billions of dollars in aid to sixteen European countries, and many historians credit the Plan with helping Western Europe recover economically far faster than it otherwise would have, while also cementing many of those same countries as reliable American allies.

Containment needed institutions to make it credible over the long run, not just a single speech. In 1949, the United States and eleven other nations formed the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, known as NATOthe North Atlantic Treaty Organization, a mutual-defense alliance built on the promise that an attack on any member is an attack on all., a mutual-defense alliance built on the promise that an attack on any member would be treated as an attack on all. NATO gave containment teeth: any Soviet move against Western Europe would now risk war with the combined military power of the whole alliance, not just the country directly attacked. NATO began with twelve members in 1949 and has since grown to include more than thirty countries across Europe and North America, several of them former Soviet allies or even former Soviet republics, an expansion Russia has repeatedly protested as a broken promise.

Containment was tested almost immediately. In 1948 and 1949, the Soviet Union blockaded West Berlin, cutting off road and rail access in an attempt to force the Western powers out of the city entirely. Rather than fighting through the blockade, the United States and its allies organized the Berlin Airlift, flying in food, fuel, and supplies around the clock for nearly a year until the Soviets finally lifted the blockade, a vivid early example of containment in action: resisting Soviet pressure firmly, without triggering a direct military confrontation.

The scale of the airlift was enormous. At its peak, American and British aircraft landed in West Berlin roughly every three minutes, delivering an average of nearly five thousand tons of food, coal, and supplies each day for almost eleven months straight. West Berlin never fell, and the Soviet Union lifted the blockade in May 1949 having gained nothing it wanted and having demonstrated, unintentionally, just how far the Western allies were willing to go to hold the line without firing a shot.

Containment's logic connected directly to what you read last week about Vietnam. Many American policymakers embraced a related idea called the domino theorythe fear that if one nation fell to communism, its neighbors would topple in turn, like a row of falling dominoes.: the fear that if one nation fell to communism, its neighbors would topple in turn, like a row of falling dominoes. This theory helped justify escalating American involvement in Vietnam through the 1960s, on the reasoning that containing communism there was necessary to prevent a wider collapse across Southeast Asia. Whether the domino theory accurately predicted what would actually happen if Vietnam fell remains debated among historians to this day.

Containment's defenders point to its eventual outcome as vindication. The Soviet Union itself collapsed in 1991, dissolving into fifteen independent nations, without the direct military clash between American and Soviet forces that many had feared throughout the Cold War. Whether containment caused that outcome, or whether the Soviet system would have collapsed under its own internal pressures regardless of American policy, remains a genuine historical debate, but the fact that the confrontation ended without a third world war is itself a significant measure of the policy's success by its own stated goal.

NATO's mutual-defense promise is written into the treaty's Article 5. In a detail many people find surprising, Article 5 has been formally invoked only once in NATO's entire history, and not to defend a European member against Soviet or Russian aggression, the scenario NATO was built for. It was invoked on September 12, 2001, the day after the terrorist attacks on the United States, when NATO's other members declared the attacks on American soil an attack on the alliance as a whole.
