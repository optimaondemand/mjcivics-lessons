📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does the American constitutional system look like from the outside, compared to a government built on a genuinely different premise?
This course closes where a comparison is most useful: not comparing the American system to an abstract theory, but to a real government built on a genuinely different premise. Communist theory, as practiced in the Soviet Union under Joseph Stalin, replaced private property and market prices with state ownership and centrally planned production: the Five-Year Plans meant to industrialize the country rapidly under government direction.

Agriculture was forcibly collectivizedkuh-lek-tiv-ih-ZAY-shunthe forced merging of independent peasant farms into state-run collectives.: independent peasant farms were merged into state-run collectives, often at gunpoint, over the fierce resistance of the peasants who owned them. The result, in 1932 and 1933, was a catastrophic famine across Ukraine and southern Russia that killed millions: a famine largely caused by state grain-requisition policy, not natural disaster, and one the Soviet government worked hard to deny and conceal from the outside world.

Some Western journalists stationed in Moscow, dependent on continued Soviet access for their careers, echoed the denial. A young Welsh journalist named Gareth Jones did not. Jones walked through Ukrainian villages himself, outside the official Soviet press tours, and reported what he actually saw.

The most prominent journalist echoing Soviet denials was Walter Duranty of the New York Times, whose dispatches dismissed reports of famine as exaggerated even as he privately told other officials that millions were likely dying. Duranty had already won a Pulitzer Prize for earlier Soviet reporting, and his public standing made his denials far more damaging to Jones's credibility than they should have been. Decades later, the Times itself published an editor's note acknowledging that Duranty's famine reporting had seriously understated the truth.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary
Holodomor (hoh-loh-doh-MOR)
Ukrainian for “death by hunger”; the 1932–33 famine across Ukraine and southern Russia, caused largely by state grain-requisition policy.
kulaks (KOO-lahks)
a term originally meaning a relatively prosperous farmer, used by Soviet authorities to label almost anyone who resisted collectivization.

Ukrainians today call this famine the Holodomorhoh-loh-doh-MORUkrainian for “death by hunger”; the 1932-33 famine across Ukraine and southern Russia, caused largely by state grain-requisition policy., a word meaning roughly “death by hunger,” and many governments around the world have since formally recognized it as a genocide deliberately inflicted on the Ukrainian people, though the exact intent behind Soviet policy remains debated among historians. Gareth Jones did not live to see this recognition. He was killed in 1935 while reporting in Mongolia, under circumstances some historians suspect involved Soviet intelligence, though this has never been definitively proven. He was thirty years old.

Historians' estimates of the death toll vary, but most place deaths in Ukraine alone somewhere between three and five million people, with additional deaths across other famine-affected Soviet regions. One especially harsh policy made the famine deadlier than it might otherwise have been: the Soviet government restricted internal travel through an internal passport system, and specifically blocked starving peasants in the worst-hit famine regions from leaving to search for food elsewhere, even as grain continued to be exported abroad from the same country experiencing mass starvation.

Soviet grain exports did not stop during the famine's worst months. The government kept shipping grain abroad to earn foreign currency for industrial machinery, even as its own citizens starved in the very regions that grew it. This decision, more than any single fact about weather or harvest size, is why many historians reject the idea that the disaster was simply an unavoidable natural event rather than a consequence of deliberate state policy.

It is worth distinguishing communism as a theory from communism as it was actually practiced in the Soviet Union. Karl Marx, the nineteenth-century philosopher whose writing inspired the movement, envisioned an eventual stateless, classless society where resources were shared according to need. In practice, every large-scale attempt to build a communist state, including the Soviet Union, instead produced a powerful, centralized state controlling the economy and much of daily life, not the stateless vision Marx had described. This gap between communist theory and its real-world implementation is itself worth remembering whenever comparing any political theory to the government actually built in its name.

The peasants who resisted collectivization most fiercely were often labeled kulaksKOO-lahksa term originally meaning a relatively prosperous farmer, used by Soviet authorities to label almost anyone who resisted collectivization., a term originally meaning a relatively prosperous farmer but used by Soviet authorities to describe almost anyone who opposed handing over their land and livestock to state control. The government's campaign against kulaks, called dekulakization, involved seizing their property, exiling entire families to remote regions of the Soviet Union, and in many cases executing those identified as kulak “class enemies.” This targeting of a specific group, defined more by their resistance to state policy than by any objective measure of wealth, foreshadowed the same pattern of labeling and punishing perceived enemies that the Gulag system would later apply on an even larger scale.

Estimates suggest more than a million people were formally classified as kulaks and deported during the early 1930s, most of them sent to remote regions of Siberia or Central Asia with little preparation for the journey or the harsh climate awaiting them. Many died along the way or in the difficult years that followed, a toll historians usually count separately from the famine's own death total.

📚 PART 2 · A COMPARISON FROM THE OUTSIDE4 min
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: why might an independent journalist be more reliable than one dependent on continued government access to do his job? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Jones's dispatches, published in the Western press in 1933, directly contradicted the Soviet government's own claims and those of journalists willing to overlook the evidence. This is what the enumerated-powers principle from Week 9 and the free press protections from Week 12 look like from the outside: a government that answers to no independent legislature, no independent courts, and no free press has far less friction slowing its ability to hide a catastrophe of its own making.
