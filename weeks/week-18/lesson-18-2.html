📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a government can hide a famine, what else can it hide, and who is left to stop it?
A government that can hide a famine can also imprison the people who might expose it, and the Soviet Union built an entire legal and administrative system for exactly that purpose: the Gulag, the network of forced-labor camps that held millions of political prisoners, dissidents, and ordinary citizens swept up on fabricated or minor charges across the decades of Soviet rule.

2

📚 PART 1 · A CAMP SYSTEM WITH NO CHECK ON IT

No independent judiciary, of the kind you studied in Week 11, existed to check whether an arrest or a sentence had any real basis; the courts served the Communist Party's interests rather than functioning as a check on state power.

⚡ FLIP THE CARD
The Gulag
The Soviet network of forced-labor camps holding millions of political prisoners, dissidents, and ordinary citizens on fabricated or minor charges, with no independent judiciary to check the arrests.
⚡ FLIP THE CARD
Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn
A Soviet Army veteran arrested in 1945 for privately criticizing Stalin in a letter to a friend; spent eight years in the Gulag; wrote The Gulag Archipelago, published in the West in 1973.

Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, a Soviet Army veteran arrested in 1945 for privately criticizing Stalin in a letter to a friend, spent eight years in the Gulag system himself. His multi-volume work The Gulag Archipelago, smuggled out of the Soviet Union and published in the West in 1973, was the first systematic account to document the camp system's scale and mechanics from the perspective of someone who had actually survived it, drawing on his own experience and testimony he gathered from hundreds of fellow former prisoners.

📚 PART 2 · A SYSTEM THAT CAN CORRECT ITSELF, AND ONE THAT CANNOT
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
This course has not pretended the American system is perfect. Module 4 examined Plessy v. Ferguson as a genuine constitutional failure. What matters is whether a system has the tools to correct its own failures.

Solzhenitsyn's account matters for this course's closing comparison because it makes concrete what the Bill of Rights and judicial review from Modules 3 and 4 exist to prevent: arrest without meaningful due process, courts that serve the state rather than the law, and a press too controlled to report any of it honestly. The difference between a system capable of correcting its own failures through an independent judiciary and free press, and one with neither, is precisely the difference this week's two primary sources make visible.
