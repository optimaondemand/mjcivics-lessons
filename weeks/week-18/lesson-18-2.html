📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If a government can hide a famine, what else can it hide, and who is left to stop it?
A government that can hide a famine can also imprison the people who might expose it, and the Soviet Union built an entire legal and administrative system for exactly that purpose: the Gulag, the network of forced-labor camps that held millions of political prisoners, dissidents, and ordinary citizens swept up on fabricated or minor charges across the decades of Soviet rule.

🌐 Foreign Language Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 18.1, plus new terms for the Soviet camp system.

Gulag (GOO-lahg)
the Soviet network of forced-labor camps that held millions of political prisoners and ordinary citizens on fabricated or minor charges.
Holodomor (hoh-loh-doh-MOR)
Ukrainian for “death by hunger”; the 1932–33 Soviet famine.
kulaks (KOO-lahks)
a term used by Soviet authorities to label almost anyone who resisted collectivization.
Solzhenitsyn (sohl-zhuh-NEET-sin)
a Soviet Army veteran and Gulag survivor whose book The Gulag Archipelago documented the camp system's scale and mechanics.

No independent judiciary, of the kind you studied in Week 11, existed to check whether an arrest or a sentence had any real basis; the courts served the Communist Party's interests rather than functioning as a check on state power.

Arrests were carried out by the Soviet secret police, known at different periods by different names, most infamously the NKVD, which operated with almost no outside oversight. A special internal body called a troika, just three officials working in secret, could convict and sentence a person to years in the camps, or even execution, often in a matter of minutes, without a public trial, a defense lawyer, or any right of appeal.

His multi-volume work The Gulag Archipelago, smuggled out of the Soviet Union and published in the West in 1973, was the first systematic account to document the camp system's scale and mechanics from the perspective of someone who had actually survived it, drawing on his own experience and testimony he gathered from hundreds of fellow former prisoners.

Solzhenitsyn had already won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1970, largely for earlier work describing camp life. The publication of The Gulag Archipelago in the West prompted the Soviet government to arrest him again, strip him of citizenship, and forcibly exile him from the country in 1974. He spent nearly two decades living abroad, much of it in the United States, before finally returning to Russia in 1994, after the Soviet Union's collapse, to see the country whose history he had documented at such personal cost.

Historians estimate that some eighteen million people passed through the Gulag system between the 1930s and the 1950s, though many prisoners died from overwork, malnutrition, and brutal conditions before ever being released. The system did not run on secret police alone. Ordinary citizens frequently denounced neighbors, coworkers, and even family members to the authorities, sometimes out of genuine belief, sometimes out of fear of being denounced first, and sometimes for personal gain, such as an informer's chance to move into a denounced neighbor's now-vacant apartment. This is exactly the uncomfortable truth Solzhenitsyn's primary source excerpt names directly: the system's cruelty depended on ordinary people's participation, not solely on villains at the top.

Some Gulag camps became notorious in their own right, especially those in the far northeastern region of Kolyma, where winter temperatures could fall below negative fifty degrees Fahrenheit and prisoners were still required to meet daily labor quotas, mining gold and other minerals the Soviet state badly needed. Survival in camps like Kolyma often depended less on physical strength than on securing an indoor job, since outdoor labor in that climate killed prisoners at an especially high rate, particularly during the long winter months.

📚 PART 2 · A SYSTEM THAT CAN CORRECT ITSELF, AND ONE THAT CANNOT4 min
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
This course has not pretended the American system is perfect. Module 4 examined Plessy v. Ferguson as a genuine constitutional failure. What matters is whether a system has the tools to correct its own failures.

This is the final lesson of Quarter 2. Every institution you have studied, from Athens's Council of Five Hundred to Rome's dual executive, from Locke's natural rights to Madison's separated powers, from the Bill of Rights to the modern Supreme Court, exists in answer to the same handful of questions this course opened with in Week 1: who rules, on what authority, and what stops that rule from curdling into abuse? The Soviet Union answered those questions one way. The American Constitution answers them another. This week's readings ask you to hold both answers side by side and decide, with real evidence in hand, which one you find more convincing, and why. That decision, made with evidence rather than assumed by birth, is itself the final act of citizenship this course has been preparing you to practice.

Solzhenitsyn's account matters for this course's closing comparison because it makes concrete what the Bill of Rights and judicial review from Modules 3 and 4 exist to prevent: arrest without meaningful due process, courts that serve the state rather than the law, and a press too controlled to report any of it honestly. The difference between a system capable of correcting its own failures through an independent judiciary and free press, and one with neither, is precisely the difference this week's two primary sources make visible.

Millions of Gulag survivors were eventually released and formally rehabilitated after Stalin's death in 1953, as his successors acknowledged, carefully and only partially, that many arrests had been unjust. But that rehabilitation came only after the fact, for people who had already lost years or decades of their lives, and for the millions who died in the camps, it came too late to mean anything at all. A system with no independent check in the first place cannot promise its citizens correction in time; it can only apologize afterward, if it apologizes at all.
