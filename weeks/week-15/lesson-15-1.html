📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Could racially segregated public schools ever actually be equal?
This week closes Module 4 with the case most Americans could name if asked for only one: Brown v. Board of Education (1954). The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, guarantees every citizen "the equal protection of the laws." For nearly sixty years before Brown, the Supreme Court had permitted states to satisfy that guarantee through legally segregated public facilities, schools, railway cars, and more, under the doctrine of "separate but equal," a doctrine this week's second primary source will examine directly.

📚 PART 1 · A QUESTION SIXTY YEARS IN THE MAKING
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: do you think the Supreme Court that decided Brown v. Board of Education was unanimous, or closely divided? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Brown asked the Court to decide whether racially segregated public schools could ever actually be equal. Chief Justice Earl Warren's opinion, joined by all nine Justices, answered no. Segregated schools, Warren wrote, harmed black children regardless of whether the physical buildings, textbooks, and teacher salaries were formally equal, because the act of separation itself communicated a message of inferiority that no amount of matched resources could undo. The doctrine of "separate but equal," the Court held, "has no place" in public education. Separate was, by its nature, unequal.

📚 PART 2 · A RULING IS NOT THE SAME AS ENFORCEMENT
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A unanimous ruling from the Court did not by itself desegregate a single school the next morning; enforcement took years of further litigation, federal action, and the broader civil rights movement your later coursework will trace.

But Brown did something a legislature alone could not easily have done at the time: it declared, as a matter of constitutional law binding on every state, that segregation could no longer claim the Fourteenth Amendment's protection, correcting an error the Court itself had made fifty-eight years earlier.
