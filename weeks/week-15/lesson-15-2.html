📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 15.1, plus one new term for this week.

equal protection
the 14th Amendment's guarantee, to every citizen, of “the equal protection of the laws.”
Jim Crow laws
the system of state segregation laws, named after a racist minstrel show character, that Plessy gave constitutional cover to grow for decades.
“separate but equal”
the doctrine that legally segregated public facilities satisfied equal protection, overturned by Brown.

To understand what Brown corrected, you have to read the case it corrected, and you have to understand that Plessy v. Ferguson was not a spontaneous act of discrimination but a deliberately staged legal test. Homer Plessy, who was seven-eighths white and one-eighth black, was a member of the Comité des Citoyenskoh-mee-TAY day see-twah-YENa group of black and Creole activists in New Orleans who organized the staged legal test that became Plessy v. Ferguson. (Citizens' Committee), a group of black and Creole activists in New Orleans organized specifically to challenge Louisiana's 1890 law requiring separate railway cars by race.

The East Louisiana Railroad cooperated willingly: the law was also a financial burden on the company, which had to run extra, mostly empty cars to keep the races separated, so the railroad worked with the Comité to arrange a test case in advance. Plessy was chosen partly because he could pass as white, but the train conductor had been told beforehand to identify him and order him into the car reserved for black passengers, and a private detective, hired for the occasion, stood ready to make the actual arrest once Plessy refused to move.

The Comité des Citoyens was not a spontaneous protest; it was a funded, organized campaign. Its members, many of them Creole professionals from New Orleans's free black community, raised money through the black press to cover legal costs, and they hired Albion Tourgée, a white lawyer and former Union Army officer with a long record of defending black civil rights, to argue the case all the way to the Supreme Court. The Comité understood from the start that a single arrest was only the first step in a legal strategy built to reach the nation's highest court.

📚 PART 2 · THE RULING AND THE LONE DISSENT5 min

In Plessy v. Ferguson, the Supreme Court upheld the law anyway, ruling that "separate but equalthe doctrine that legally segregated public facilities satisfied equal protection, overturned by Brown." facilities satisfied the Fourteenth Amendment so long as the separate facilities were of comparable quality. The majority went further, dismissing the argument that forced separation itself branded black citizens as inferior, a claim, the Court wrote, that existed only "because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it."

The vote in Plessy was seven to one, with one Justice not participating in the case at all. That lopsided majority is itself worth noting, since it shows how thoroughly the legal thinking of the era, not just one or two individual judges, accepted segregation as constitutionally permissible. Plessy did not create the system of segregation laws that came to be known as Jim Crow lawsthe system of state segregation laws, named after a racist minstrel show character, that Plessy gave constitutional cover to grow for decades., named after a racist minstrel show character; those laws were already spreading across Southern and border states in the years before 1896. What Plessy did was give that entire system the Supreme Court's constitutional blessing, removing what might otherwise have been a serious legal obstacle to its growth for the next several decades.

This week's second primary source is that majority opinion, and it should be read as this course reads the three-fifths compromise or Japanese internment: honestly, as a documented failure of the American constitutional order, not softened into "one reasonable view among several."

Plessy's ruling opened the door to a wider set of Jim Crow measures far beyond railway cars. In the years after 1896, several Southern states added poll taxes, literacy tests, and so-called "grandfather clauses" that let a man vote only if his grandfather had voted, provisions written specifically to exclude black voters while technically avoiding the word "race" at all. Courts routinely upheld these measures using the same "separate but equal" logic Plessy had blessed, since each one could be defended, on its face, as applying equally to every citizen regardless of race.

⚡ FLIP CARD · CLICK TO REVEAL
One Justice saw through the majority's reasoning even at the time. Who, and what did he say?
✅ ANSWER
Justice John Marshall Harlan's lone dissent insisted that "our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens," and predicted that the decision would prove as damaging as the Court's earlier ruling in Dred Scott v. Sandford. Harlan was in the minority of one in 1896. By 1954, his reasoning, not the majority's, had become the law of the land.

Harlan's willingness to dissent alone is itself worth noticing. Before the Civil War, Harlan had come from a slaveholding Kentucky family, and by his own account his views on race changed substantially in the decades that followed. That personal change makes his lone dissent a reminder that constitutional reasoning, like a person's own understanding, can outgrow assumptions once taken for granted, the same kind of honest growth this course asks of its own students when they revisit an earlier opinion in light of better evidence.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT SIXTY YEARS TEACHES4 min
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
The sixty-year gap between Plessy and Brown is itself a civics lesson: the Supreme Court is not infallible, judicial review from Week 11 can entrench a serious wrong as readily as it can correct one, and the system's capacity for self-correction depends on later generations, later Justices, and often decades of civil-rights organizing willing to keep bringing the same fundamental question back to the Court until it is answered rightly.

The lawyer who argued Brown before the Supreme Court was Thurgood Marshall, working for the NAACP Legal Defense Fund, an organization built specifically to bring exactly this kind of carefully chosen test case through the courts, patiently, over many years. Marshall had already won a string of smaller victories chipping away at segregation in graduate schools and law schools before bringing the broader challenge to segregated primary education in Brown. Thirteen years later, in 1967, Marshall himself became the first black Justice appointed to the Supreme Court, the same institution whose earlier ruling in Plessy his own legal career had helped overturn.

Harlan's phrase "our Constitution is color-blind" did not disappear after 1896. It resurfaced across the twentieth century, and it continues to be quoted today, on more than one side of ongoing debates over how directly government may take race into account, even for remedial purposes meant to address segregation's own aftereffects. A single sentence written in dissent, by a Court's lone holdout, can outlast the majority opinion it argued against by well over a century.
