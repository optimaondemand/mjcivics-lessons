📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you had just expelled a king and never wanted to be ruled by one man again, would you hand executive power to one new leader, or split it?
Athens was not the only ancient city to overthrow a would-be tyrant and build something new. In 509 BC, a full generation before Cleisthenes's reforms had even settled into place across the Aegean, the Romans expelled their last king, Tarquin the Proud, and swore never to be ruled by a single man again. What they built instead, the res publica ("the public thing," the root of our word "republic"), would eventually govern the Mediterranean world and, two thousand years later, supply the American founders with their working vocabulary of government.

📚 PART 1 · TWO CONSULS, NOT ONE KING
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read on: if two officials each held equal power and each could veto the other, would you expect that to make government faster, or slower? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Rome's solution to the problem of executive power was strikingly different from Athens's. In the Athenian Assembly, there was no selection process at all: any citizen who chose to show up on a given day could speak and vote, on a strict first-come basis, and if the crowd exceeded what the meeting place could hold, latecomers simply went home without a vote. Only the Council of Five Hundred, discussed in Week 1, actually rotated citizens through office by lot. The Romans took neither path. They concentrated executive authority in just two officials, called Consuls, elected annually. This is the dual executive at the center of this week's discussion: two men, holding equal power, each able to veto the other's actions, each serving only one year before stepping down. If a single consul held too much ambition, his colleague could block him. If a year's term bred no lasting loyalty, no consul could entrench himself the way a king could.

📚 PART 2 · THE SENATE, THE ASSEMBLIES, AND THE TRIBUNE'S VETO

The Consuls did not rule alone. The Senate, a body of several hundred former magistrates drawn overwhelmingly from wealthy patrician families, held no formal legislative power but wielded immense influence through its control of finance, foreign policy, and its accumulated prestige (auctoritas). Below the Senate stood several popular Assemblies, where Roman citizens voted on laws and elected magistrates, and eventually the office of Tribune of the Plebs, won by the common people (plebeians) after a long political struggle with the aristocracy (patricians) known as the Struggle of the Orders. A tribune could veto almost any act of government on behalf of the plebeians: a single word, intercessio, and the action stopped.

Notice what has already emerged: monarchy's decisiveness (the consuls), aristocracy's experience (the Senate), and democracy's voice (the assemblies and tribunes), each checking the others. The Romans did not design this balance on a single afternoon; it accumulated over centuries of trial, conflict, and negotiated compromise. But by the time the Greek historian Polybius arrived in Rome in the 160s BC, he believed he was looking at something genuinely new: a constitution that combined the three classical forms of government instead of choosing between them. This week's primary sources come from Polybius and from Cicero, writing a century later as the Republic he was praising began to unravel.
