Cleisthenes and the Machinery of Direct Democracy

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If power is spread across a Council chosen partly by lot, an Assembly of thousands, and an exile vote with no formal charge, what actually holds a system like that together?
Rebuilding the tribes was Cleisthenes's own distinctive move, but it built on a longer process already under way. It was Solon, back in 594 BC, who deserves the primary credit for breaking the aristocratic families' grip on Athenian politics in the first place: his property classes opened political office to any citizen with enough wealth, aristocrat or not, and by Solon's time plenty of commoners had grown wealthy enough to qualify. Pisistratus, for all that he seized power by force, continued this same work. He kept Solon's property requirements in place while elevating wealthy and middle-class commoners and steadily pushing the old aristocratic families further from power. Cleisthenes's reforms, coming after Pisistratus's line was driven out, had a different emphasis: not breaking the aristocracy's economic grip, which Solon and Pisistratus had already accomplished, but increasing the power of the Assembly itself and expanding who could be counted as a citizen in the first place. With that background in place, here are the institutions Cleisthenes built, and the ones later reformers would still need to finish.

📚 PART 1 · THE COUNCIL OF FIVE HUNDRED
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read on: do you think a body chosen partly by election and partly by random lot would tend to be more, or less, dominated by wealthy citizens than a body chosen by election alone? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

The Council of Five Hundred (the Boule) replaced Solon's smaller Council of Four Hundred. Each year, fifty men were selected from each of the ten new tribes to serve, through a two-stage process: each deme, or local district, first held its own election to choose its candidates, an election that in practice favored wealthier and middle-class residents, since serving on the Council was time-consuming work a man who labored for wages could not easily afford to take on. Only after that internal election did the final selection happen by lot. The Council prepared the agenda for the full citizen Assembly, supervised magistrates, and handled day-to-day administration, and because it controlled that agenda, the Boule, not the Assembly, held the practical upper hand under Cleisthenes's system.

It would take another generation, and the reforms of Ephialtes and Pericles in the 460s and 450s BC, before this balance shifted: those later reformers introduced a stipend for serving on the Council, making it economically possible for the poorest citizens, the thetes, to serve at all, and only then did the Assembly truly gain the upper hand in setting its own agenda. Selection by lot, once it applied to a genuinely broad pool of citizens, is worth pausing on: the Athenians believed that rotating citizens through office by chance, rather than always electing the most persuasive or wealthy speakers, protected the system from being captured by a permanent political class.

📚 PART 2 · THE ASSEMBLY AND OSTRACISM

The Assembly: Direct Democracy Itself

The Assembly (Ekklesia) was where every adult male citizen, not a representative, the citizen himself, could speak and vote directly on war, peace, laws, and public spending. This is direct democracy: the citizens are not choosing someone to rule for them, they are ruling. Meeting on the Pnyx hill overlooking the city, thousands of Athenians would gather to debate and vote by show of hands.

Ostracism: A Safety Valve

The third institution, introduced a few years later, was ostracism: once a year, the Assembly could vote to exile any citizen for ten years, not as a criminal punishment, but as a safety valve against anyone accumulating too much personal power or popularity to threaten the democracy itself. No formal charge was needed, just enough votes.

📚 PART 3 · WHY THE COMBINATION MATTERED

Together these institutions answer one of the oldest political questions there is: who rules, and what stops that rule from curdling into tyranny again? Cleisthenes's answer, completed only later by Ephialtes and Pericles, was to distribute power widely and rotate it constantly, so that no faction could hold it long enough to abuse it. Notice what this system does not have: no independent judiciary as we would recognize one, no separation of powers in the modern sense, no written bill of rights. Athenian democracy protected liberty through participation and rotation, not through structural limits on power. When you reach the American Constitutional Convention later this quarter, watch for how differently Madison and the framers chose to answer this same question.
