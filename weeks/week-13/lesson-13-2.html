📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens when a law does not target religion directly, but still pressures someone to abandon a religious practice?
If Lemon deals with government aiding religion too closely, Sherbert v. Verner (1963) deals with the opposite half of the First Amendment's religion clauses: government burdening someone's free exercise of religion.

📚 PART 1 · SHERBERT'S CHOICE4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing three terms from Lesson 13.1, plus new terms for this week's free-exercise case.

Establishment Clause
the First Amendment promise that “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion.”
Free Exercise Clause
the First Amendment promise protecting against laws “prohibiting the free exercise” of religion.
Lemon test
the three-part test asking whether a government action has a secular purpose, neither advances nor inhibits religion, and avoids “excessive government entanglement.”
Sherbert test
the rule that once a law substantially burdens someone's free exercise of religion, government must show a “compelling state interest” to justify it.
strict scrutiny
the highest level of legal review American courts use, requiring a “compelling state interest” to justify a law.

Adell Sherbert, a Seventh-day Adventist in South Carolina, was fired after she refused to work on Saturdays, her faith's Sabbath. When she applied for unemployment benefits, the state denied her claim, ruling that she had turned down "suitable work" without good cause.

South Carolina's law let a worker turn down a job for "good cause" without losing benefits, but state officials had never before counted a religious Sabbath objection as good cause the way the law was applied to Sherbert. She had worked at the same textile mill for over thirty years before it switched to a six-day week that included Saturdays, and no other work in her area was available that did not require Saturday work. She was not asking for a brand-new right; she was asking why the state's own "good cause" exception did not cover a lifelong religious practice the same way it might cover other personal circumstances.

⚡ FLIP CARD · CLICK TO REVEAL
What did the Supreme Court decide, and on what reasoning?
✅ ANSWER
The Court ruled that denying benefits placed real pressure on Sherbert to abandon her Sabbath in order to receive a government benefit, "the same kind of burden" Justice Brennan wrote as a direct fine for Saturday worship. The Court held that once a law substantially burdens free exercise, government must show a "compelling state interest" to justify that burden, and South Carolina had shown none.
📚 PART 2 · THE SHERBERT TEST7 min
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
This standard, the Sherbert test, gave free-exercise claims real teeth for the next several decades: once a burden on religious practice is shown, the government carries the heavy burden of proving a compelling state interest, not merely a reasonable one.

Lawyers have a name for this level of justification: strict scrutinythe highest level of legal review American courts use, requiring a “compelling state interest” to justify a law., the highest level of legal review American courts use for any law. A law facing strict scrutiny must serve a compelling government interest, and must be narrowly tailored so that it burdens no more of the protected activity than genuinely necessary. Most laws never face this level of review at all; courts usually ask only whether a law is reasonable. Sherbert placed free-exercise burdens inside this narrow, demanding category, which is exactly why the Sherbert test mattered so much to religious minorities who might otherwise find their sincere practices simply inconvenient for an otherwise neutral law to accommodate.

Notice why this pattern matters most for religious minorities specifically. Laws are usually written with the majority's ordinary schedule and practices already built in, without anyone necessarily noticing: a work week built around a five-day, Monday-through-Friday schedule quietly assumes a Sunday, not a Saturday, sabbath. A Christian worker observing Sunday rarely needs a special exception, since the ordinary calendar already accommodates that practice by default. A Seventh-day Adventist or an observant Jew keeping a Saturday sabbath needs the law to bend somewhere it would not otherwise bend on its own. The Sherbert test existed, in part, to make sure the Constitution's protection did not quietly favor whichever religious practice happened to already match the majority's calendar.

The Sherbert test's reach did not last forever either. In 1990, in Employment Division v. Smith, the Supreme Court significantly narrowed it. Alfred Smith and Galen Black were fired from their jobs at a private drug rehabilitation clinic after their employer learned they had used peyote, a hallucinogenic cactus, as part of a religious ceremony of the Native American Church. Oregon denied them unemployment benefits for work-related misconduct, and this time the Supreme Court sided with the state, holding that a neutral law applied equally to everyone, religious use included, does not need the compelling-interest justification Sherbert had demanded. The key difference the Court drew was between a law that singles out religious practice for special burden and a law that applies evenly to religious and non-religious conduct alike; Oregon's drug law banned peyote for everyone, not specifically for the Native American Church, which the majority treated as enough to avoid strict scrutiny altogether.

Congress disagreed with this narrower reading and responded in 1993 by passing the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, which restored something close to the Sherbert standard for cases involving federal law. That Act has had real, visible effects since: in Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores (2014), the Supreme Court used the Act's restored compelling-interest standard to rule that a closely held family business could object, on religious grounds, to providing certain contraceptive coverage the federal government otherwise required under a separate law. The Sherbert-versus-Smith debate over how much protection free exercise deserves did not end with either case; it simply moved from constitutional law directly into the ordinary statutes Congress writes. A Court ruling narrows a protection, Congress writes a statute to restore it, and a later case tests how far that statute itself reaches: constitutional rights in practice are rarely settled once and for all, but worked out continuously, across decades, by all three branches acting on each other in turn.

Read together, Lemon and Sherbert map the two edges of the same clause: government may not push religion on citizens through excessive entanglement, and it may not push citizens away from their religion through indirect penalties either. The First Amendment's religion clauses, easy to read as a flat prohibition, turn out to require two distinct, carefully calibrated tests just to apply consistently, a pattern you will see repeat in next week's Fourth, Fifth, and Sixth Amendment cases.
