📚 PART 1 · TWO PROMISES IN ONE SENTENCE
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing three terms from Lesson 12.2, plus new terms for this week's First Amendment case.

Bill of Rights
the first ten amendments to the Constitution, ratified in 1791, restraining what the federal government may do to individual rights.
Establishment Clause
the First Amendment promise that “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion.”
Free Exercise Clause
the First Amendment promise protecting against laws “prohibiting the free exercise” of religion.
incorporation (in-kor-por-AY-shun)
the gradual process of applying most Bill of Rights protections against state governments too.
Lemon test
the three-part test from Lemon v. Kurtzman asking whether a government action has a secular purpose, neither advances nor inhibits religion, and avoids “excessive government entanglement.”
militia (muh-LISH-uh)
a body of ordinary citizens, trained and organized rather than professional soldiers.

The First Amendment's religion clauses are really two separate promises stacked in one sentence: the Establishment Clause ("Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion") and the Free Exercise Clause ("or prohibiting the free exercise thereof"). Reading last week's text was easy; applying it to a real dispute is much harder. What exactly counts as government "establishing" a religion, short of literally creating an official state church?

That question reached the Supreme Court in 1971 in Lemon v. Kurtzman, after Pennsylvania and Rhode Island passed laws using state funds to help pay the salaries of teachers at religious schools for secular subjects like math and science. Chief Justice Warren Burger's opinion for the Court struck down the laws.

📚 PART 2 · THE LEMON TEST
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
In doing so, Chief Justice Burger's opinion distilled decades of earlier religion-clause cases into a single three-part test, since known simply as the Lemon test: a government action touching religion is constitutional only if it has a secular purpose, if its primary effect neither advances nor inhibits religion, and if it does not foster "excessive government entanglement" between church and state.

The test takes its name simply from the case, named for Alton Lemon, a parent and taxpayer who challenged Pennsylvania's law, not from any connection to the fruit or to the English idiom for a bad product. Courts used it for decades to evaluate government-sponsored prayer in public schools, religious displays on government property, and released-time religious education programs: a Nativity scene on city hall steps, a moment of silence at the start of a school day, a cross on a war memorial each forced courts to ask the same three questions, purpose, effect, and entanglement, even though the facts varied enormously from case to case.

The Lemon test's dominance did not last forever. Later Supreme Court decisions gradually criticized and narrowed it, and in 2022, in Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, the Court stated plainly that the Lemon test no longer governs Establishment Clause cases, replacing it with a test based more directly on historical practices and understandings. That case involved a public high school football coach who knelt to pray at the fifty-yard line after games, and the district's decision to stop renewing his contract over the practice. The Court ruled in the coach's favor, holding that both his free exercise and free speech rights protected this personal, quiet prayer, and explicitly rejected the district's Lemon-based argument that allowing it would look like the school endorsed his religion. Even a widely used judicial test can eventually be replaced by a later Court that reads the Constitution differently.

Consider why the entanglement problem worried the Court so much in the actual Lemon case. To make sure a state-funded math teacher at a Catholic school was not quietly weaving religious content into her lessons, the state would need ongoing inspections, reporting requirements, and monitoring of exactly what happened inside that classroom. That ongoing oversight is itself the “excessive entanglement” the third prong worries about, not the funding alone, but the deep, continuous government involvement in a religious institution's daily operations that policing the funding would require. The lesson extends well beyond religion cases specifically: whenever the Constitution states a broad principle in a single sentence, real enforcement almost always requires courts, over time, to build a more detailed operating test underneath that sentence. The words rarely change; the tests interpreting those words change constantly, as later courts revisit, refine, and sometimes completely replace the frameworks earlier courts built.
