📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 9.1, plus new terms for what Congress can and cannot do.

enumerated powers (ee-NOO-mer-ay-ted POW-erz)
the doctrine that a national legislature may only exercise the powers specifically named for it in the text, not simply assumed.
House of Representatives
the chamber of Congress apportioned by state population and elected every two years.
Necessary and Proper Clause
the clause letting Congress make laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers; critics call it the “elastic clause.”
police power
the broad power to regulate for public health, safety, and morals, which the Constitution leaves to the states rather than granting to Congress.
Senate (SEH-nit)
the chamber of Congress giving every state two Senators regardless of population.

📚 PART 2 · THE CLAUSE THAT WON'T STOP ARGUING4 min

The list closes with a clause that has generated more constitutional argument than almost any other sentence in the document. Congress may “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers.” This is called the Necessary and Proper Clausethe clause letting Congress make laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers; critics call it the “elastic clause.”, and its critics sometimes call it the “elastic clause.” It lets Congress choose the specific means for exercising its enumerated ends, without the Constitution having to spell out every conceivable law in advance.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Read narrowly, the Necessary and Proper Clause is a modest housekeeping clause. Read broadly, it lets Congress reach into territory its critics say was never actually delegated to it in the first place. This is a live, ongoing argument about exactly how much elasticity the elastic clause was meant to have.
📚 PART 3 · WHAT SECTION 8 DOES NOT GRANT4 min

Notice what this section does not grant. Congress has no general “police powerthe broad power to regulate for public health, safety, and morals, which the Constitution leaves to the states rather than granting to Congress.” to regulate for the health, safety, and morals of the public. The states retained that broad power for themselves. This is the same logic of limited, divided government you traced in Week 4 through Montesquieu, and in the Preamble's structure in Week 6. A legislature that can point to a specific textual grant for everything it does is a legislature that can be checked, in principle, by anyone who can actually read the list.

Consider a concrete contrast. Congress can regulate interstate trucking, since that falls under its enumerated power to regulate commerce among the states. But Congress cannot, on its own authority, set the speed limit on a purely local city street, license hairdressers, or decide who may get married, since none of those powers appears anywhere in Article I, Section 8's list. States handle all of that under their own general police power instead. This is exactly the kind of sorting you will practice in this week's VR activity.

📚 PART 4 · WHY A LIST AT ALL, AND WHAT McCULLOCH v. MARYLAND SETTLED4 min

It is worth naming why the framers chose an enumerated list at all, rather than simply trusting Congress with general lawmaking authority the way Parliament had traditionally worked in Britain. A list can be checked against. If Congress passes a law and someone believes it has no basis anywhere in Article I, Section 8, that person has a specific, concrete argument to make in court, not just a vague complaint about overreach. This is part of what makes judicial review, which you will study in Week 11, possible at all: courts need an actual written standard to measure a law against, and the enumerated-powers list is exactly that standard for federal legislation.

The most famous real-world test of this clause came in 1819, when the Supreme Court decided McCulloch v. Maryland. The Court ruled that Congress had the power to charter a national bank, even though “national bank” appears nowhere in Article I's enumerated list, because a bank was a reasonable and appropriate means of carrying out powers Congress clearly did have, like collecting taxes and borrowing money. That case is not one of this week's assigned primary sources, but it is worth knowing as the classic illustration of just how much weight this single clause can carry. It is also a preview of a bigger question this course will return to directly in Week 11: who ultimately decides what the Constitution's own words mean, when reasonable people read the same list and disagree about how far it stretches?

This week's three Extension readings, on the rule of law, on what states owe each other, and on how hard the Constitution is to amend, all share a common thread with Section 8's enumerated list. Each one is a different piece of the same overall project: a government whose power is written down in advance, distributed on purpose, and changeable only through a deliberately demanding process, rather than a government whose power simply expands to fill whatever room is available.
