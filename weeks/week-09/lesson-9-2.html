Lesson 9.2
The Powers Congress Actually Has
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If Congress can only do what the Constitution actually lists, what exactly is on that list, and what happens at its edges?
Structure is only half of Article I. The other half, laid out in Section 8, is a list: the framers' attempt to write down, clause by clause, exactly what Congress is and is not allowed to do.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define enumerated powers and explain why Congress is limited to the powers the Constitution names.
2
I will be able to explain what the Necessary and Proper Clause does, and why it is called the “elastic clause.”
3
I will be able to distinguish Congress's enumerated powers from a state legislature's general police power.

📚 PART 1 · A LIST, NOT A BLANK CHECK
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: do you think Congress can pass any law it judges useful, or only laws that fall under specific powers named in the text? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

This is the doctrine of enumerated powers: a national legislature may only exercise the powers specifically granted to it in the text, unlike a state legislature, which under its own constitution may generally do anything not specifically forbidden. Congress can lay and collect taxes, borrow money, regulate commerce between the states and with foreign nations, coin money, raise armies, and declare war, each power named individually, not assumed.

📚 PART 2 · THE CLAUSE THAT WON'T STOP ARGUING

The list closes with a clause that has generated more constitutional argument than almost any other sentence in the document: Congress may “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers.” Called the Necessary and Proper Clause (and, by its critics, the “elastic clause”), it lets Congress choose the means for exercising its enumerated ends without the Constitution having to spell out every conceivable law in advance.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Read narrowly, the Necessary and Proper Clause is a modest housekeeping clause. Read broadly, it lets Congress reach into territory its critics say was never actually delegated to it. That gap between the two readings is a live argument, not a settled one.
📚 PART 3 · WHAT SECTION 8 DOES NOT GRANT

Notice what this section does not grant: a general police power to regulate for the health, safety, and morals of the public, which the states retained for themselves. This is the same logic of limited, divided government you traced in Week 4 through Montesquieu and in the Preamble's structure in Week 6: a legislature that can point to a specific textual grant for everything it does is a legislature that can be checked, in principle, by anyone who can read the list.
