📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Voting is the civic obligation everyone talks about. What are the other two, and why do they matter just as much?
Module 4 studied rights: things government may not take from a citizen. This module turns to the other half of citizenship: obligations and responsibilities a citizen owes back to the political community that protects those rights. Voting is the most familiar example, and the one most often discussed. But American civic life has always asked more of citizens than casting a ballot every few years. Two other duties matter just as much: sitting on a jury when summoned, and, at various points in American history, serving in the armed forces when the nation called.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why voting, jury duty, and military service all count as civic obligations, not just rights a citizen may choose to use.
2
I will be able to explain why the Sixth Amendment's jury guarantee only functions in practice if citizens actually agree to serve.
3
I will be able to describe the circumstances and immediate effect of Roosevelt's December 8, 1941 address to Congress.

📚 PART 1 · JURY DUTY, A GUARANTEE THAT NEEDS VOLUNTEERS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: does jury duty feel like a real civic "obligation" to you in the same way voting or military service does, or does it feel like something lighter? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Jury duty may feel like the least dramatic of the three, but a constitutional guarantee is only as real as the citizens willing to staff it. The Sixth Amendment you encountered in Week 14 guarantees an accused person the right to an impartial jury and the "Assistance of Counsel," but neither promise means anything unless citizens actually show up to serve. That is the same insight behind Miranda's procedural safeguards: a right that exists only on paper, with no one to enforce or staff it, protects no one.

📚 PART 2 · A NATION MOVES FROM PEACE TO WAR
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A declaration of war is a specific constitutional act, distinct from the fighting itself, that Congress alone has the power to make.

Military service asks the most of a citizen, and American history has not always asked evenly. This week's first primary source drops you into the moment military obligation became urgent for an entire generation: President Franklin Roosevelt's address to Congress on December 8, 1941, one day after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor. Notice how quickly this single speech moves the country from peace to a declared state of war, and how directly it would shortly translate into a national draft calling millions of young Americans to serve.
