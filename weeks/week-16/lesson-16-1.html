📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Voting is the civic obligation everyone talks about. What are the other two, and why do they matter just as much?
Module 4 studied rights: things government may not take from a citizen. This module turns to the other half of citizenship: obligations and responsibilities a citizen owes back to the political community that protects those rights. Voting is the most familiar example, and the one most often discussed. But American civic life has always asked more of citizens than casting a ballot every few years. Two other duties matter just as much: sitting on a jury when summoned, and, at various points in American history, serving in the armed forces when the nation called.

Voting itself has not always meant the same thing in American history. The original Constitution left voting qualifications almost entirely to the states, and most states initially limited the vote to white male property owners. Later amendments steadily widened this circle. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) barred denying the vote based on race, though many states evaded it for decades through discriminatory practices later struck down by courts and Congress. The Nineteenth Amendment (1920) guaranteed women the vote nationwide. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment (1971) lowered the voting age to eighteen, driven substantially by the argument that a man old enough to be drafted to fight in Vietnam was old enough to vote on the policies sending him there. Each expansion answered the same underlying question this week's other two obligations raise: who, exactly, counts as a full member of the political community, obligated to serve it and entitled to a voice in it?

Congress and the states closed one more gap in 1964, when the Twenty-Fourth Amendment banned poll taxes in federal elections, one of the very tools some states had used for decades to keep poor citizens, disproportionately black citizens, from voting at all. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 went further still, authorizing federal oversight of elections in states with a history of discrimination and effectively ending literacy tests as a voting requirement nationwide. Together, these two measures did for voting what this week's lesson argues jury duty and military service still ask of every citizen directly: they turned a right written on paper into a right citizens could actually exercise.

Jury duty may feel like the least dramatic of the three, but a constitutional guarantee is only as real as the citizens willing to staff it. The Sixth Amendment you encountered in Week 14 guarantees an accused person the right to an impartial jury and the "Assistance of Counsel," but neither promise means anything unless citizens actually show up to serve. That is the same insight behind Miranda's procedural safeguards: a right that exists only on paper, with no one to enforce or staff it, protects no one.

Jury service historically tracked the same exclusions as voting. For much of American history, only white men could serve on juries in many states. The Supreme Court struck down explicit racial exclusion from juries in Strauder v. West Virginia (1880), though in practice, discriminatory jury selection persisted for decades afterward through less explicit means. Women were not guaranteed the right to serve on federal juries nationwide until 1957, and some states continued to exempt women from jury service, often at their own request, well into the 1970s. The right to serve on a jury and the right to be judged by one's fellow citizens turn out to be two sides of the very same coin.

In practice, jury summons arrive through the mail, usually drawn from voter registration rolls or driver's license records. Ignoring a jury summons entirely is not simply impolite; it is against the law in most jurisdictions, and can result in a fine or even a contempt-of-court charge. Courts do excuse people from service for genuine hardship, illness, or an unavoidable conflict, but the underlying expectation remains: this is treated as a legal duty, not merely a polite request. 📚 PART 2 · A NATION MOVES FROM PEACE TO WAR4 min
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A declaration of war is a specific constitutional act, distinct from the fighting itself, that Congress alone has the power to make.

Military service asks the most of a citizen, and American history has not always asked evenly. This week's first primary source drops you into the moment military obligation became urgent for an entire generation: President Franklin Roosevelt's address to Congress on December 8, 1941, one day after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor. Notice how quickly this single speech moves the country from peace to a declared state of war, and how directly it would shortly translate into a national draft calling millions of young Americans to serve.

The attack itself was devastating by any measure: more than 2,400 Americans killed in a single morning, eight battleships damaged or sunk, and nearly two hundred aircraft destroyed, many of them before they ever left the ground. Roosevelt's speech to Congress the next day lasted barely seven minutes, remarkably short for a request asking an entire nation to enter a world war, but Congress declared war on Japan within an hour of his finishing it, with only one dissenting vote across both chambers combined.

The same attack that produced this speech also led the government, within months, to order the forced relocation and internment of Japanese Americans, a decision this course has already examined as a documented failure of civil liberties in wartime. Roosevelt's address itself asked only for a declaration of war against the nations responsible, not for anything resembling internment. Keep both facts in view together: a single moment in history can produce one decision future generations honor and another they condemn, sometimes within the very same administration.
