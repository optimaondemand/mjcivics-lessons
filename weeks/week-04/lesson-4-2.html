📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can any government at all, however it is structured, be reconciled with genuine freedom?
If Montesquieu asked how power should be structured, the Genevan philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau, writing his On the Social Contract in 1762, asked a more unsettling question: can any government at all be reconciled with genuine freedom? Rousseau opens his book with a famous line: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." His task is to explain how legitimate chains, legitimate political authority, could ever be compatible with the freedom people are born with.

📚 PART 2 · THE GENERAL WILL

Rousseau's answer builds on the social contract tradition you have already studied in Locke, but pushes it in a distinctive direction. He argued that when individuals unite into a political community, they do not simply trade obedience for protection, as Locke's account might suggest. Instead, each person gives themselves entirely to the whole community and, in exchange, becomes a participating author of the laws that govern everyone, including themselves. The result, Rousseau claimed, is something he called the general will: not simply what most people happen to want at a given moment, but the collective judgment of the community about the common good, which every citizen has a share in forming.

📚 PART 3 · FORCED TO BE FREE?

This is a genuinely radical idea, and not an entirely comfortable one. Because the general will represents the whole community's judgment about the common good, Rousseau argued that someone who disobeys a law made this way is not simply defying the majority; he is acting against his own truer, more rational self, the self that participates in the collective sovereign. Rousseau put this starkly: society may "force him to be free." Critics have long worried that this idea, taken too far, could justify a majority (or whoever claims to speak for it) overriding an individual's rights in the name of the "real" common good; this is a very different worry from Locke's, whose theory treats individual natural rights as a line no government, however popular, may cross. Keep both worries in mind as you continue through this course: the American framers were reading Rousseau's contemporaries, but their constitutional design leans decisively toward Locke's caution about power, not Rousseau's confidence in collective will.
