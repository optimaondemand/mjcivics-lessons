📚 PART 1 · A NOBLEMAN'S QUESTION

Locke and Hobbes asked whether government is legitimate at all, and on what terms. The French nobleman, Baron de Montesquieu, writing in 1748, asked a narrower but equally consequential question: even a legitimate government can turn tyrannical if it is organized the wrong way. His answer would eventually give the American Constitution its most basic architecture.

📚 PART 2 · THREE POWERS, KEPT APART
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before reading Montesquieu's reasoning in full: do you think it is more dangerous for one official to hold all three powers of government, or for that same official to simply have a lot of money and social influence? (No wrong answer, just think it through.)

Studying England, Generalizing a Theory

Montesquieu studied England's government, which by his time had developed a rough separation between the power to make laws (Parliament), the power to carry them out (the Crown and its ministers), and the power to judge disputes under the law (the courts). He generalized this into a theory: political liberty requires that legislative, executive, and judicial power be kept in separate hands. His reasoning was blunt: whoever writes the laws, enforces the laws, and judges the laws all at once has no reason to write laws that limit himself, and no independent check exists to stop him from abusing any of the three powers. Combine any two of the three, Montesquieu warned, and arbitrary rule becomes likely; combine all three, and it becomes almost certain.

📚 PART 3 · FROM THEORY TO THE AMERICAN CONSTITUTION

This is the concept behind this week's discussion focus, "the three branches of government," and it will echo through the rest of this course. You have not yet studied the specific powers of the American Congress, President, or federal courts; that detailed study comes in Module 3. For now, hold onto the underlying principle Montesquieu is establishing: liberty is protected not by trusting good rulers to behave well, but by structurally separating power so that no single person or body can ever hold all of it. When Madison later defended the Constitution's design in the Federalist Papers, he was arguing, point by point, that the framers had built exactly the separation Montesquieu called for.

⚡ TRY IT · HOW LIKELY IS ARBITRARY RULE?
For each scenario, use Montesquieu's own warning to judge how likely arbitrary rule becomes.
1. One official holds only legislative power (writes the laws); separate people hold executive and judicial power.
2. One official holds both legislative and executive power (writing the laws and enforcing them).
3. One official holds legislative, executive, AND judicial power, all three at once.
