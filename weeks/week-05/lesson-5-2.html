Declaring independence was one problem. Building a government to replace what was thrown off turned out to be a much harder one.

📚 PART 1 · THE PAMPHLET THAT CHANGED MINDS5 min

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Common Sense sold more copies, relative to the colonial population, than any American publication before or since. Before reading why it worked, what do you think made plain, forceful language more persuasive to ordinary readers than a formal political argument? (No wrong answer.)

He published it anonymously at first. It sold more copies, compared to the size of the colonial population, than any publication in American history before or since. Earlier colonial writing had often argued narrowly about specific taxes or specific acts of Parliament. Paine attacked something much bigger: the very idea of hereditary monarchy itself, and the assumption that the colonies owed permanent loyalty to a king across an ocean. Paine wrote in plain, forceful language that ordinary readers could easily quote back to each other. By the time Congress took up Jefferson's draft five months later, Paine had already done much of the persuading. Historians estimate Common Sense sold somewhere between one hundred thousand and one hundred fifty thousand copies within its first few months alone, in a population of only about two and a half million free colonists. A modern bestseller would need to sell tens of millions of copies to match that same reach relative to today's much larger population. Paine's pamphlet also draws a sharp line between society and government, an idea you will see directly in this week's primary source excerpt. Paine argues that society comes from people's wants, their natural desire to cooperate and help each other. Government, by contrast, comes from human wickedness, the need for some way to restrain people who would otherwise take advantage of others. This is a strikingly Hobbesian idea about why government exists at all, even though Paine's ultimate argument, that free countries should be ruled by law rather than by any king, lines up much more closely with Locke's caution about unchecked power. Notice, too, how this small comparison rewards careful reading: two thinkers can share an assumption about human nature while still landing on very different conclusions about what to do about it. Keep this in mind as you read Paine's excerpt for yourself this week, and watch for the moment where he turns from describing human nature to prescribing an actual form of government.

📚 PART 2 · A GOVERNMENT DESIGNED TO BE WEAK4 min

But declaring independence and building a functioning government were two separate problems. The second problem proved far harder than the first. In November 1777, Congress adopted the Articles of Confederation as the new nation's first constitution. All thirteen states formally approved it by 1781. The colonists had just fought a war against what they saw as an overreaching central government. Because of this, the framers of the Articles built a purposefully weak one. It was called a “confederation(kun-fed-er-AY-shun)a loose alliance of independent states, with a weak central government that depends on the states for most real power.,” meaning a loose alliance of independent states. The national Congress it created had no power to tax. It had no power to regulate trade between the states. It had no national court system and no national executive to enforce its own laws. It also required the unanimous consent of all thirteen states just to make any single amendment. Each state had exactly one vote in Congress, no matter how many people lived there. Tiny Delaware carried the same weight as much larger Virginia. This equal-vote structure protected small states from being outvoted by large ones. But it also meant a bare majority of small states could block action favored by states representing most of the country's actual population.

📚 PART 3 · WHEN WEAKNESS BECOMES DANGEROUS4 min

Within a few years, the consequences of this weakness became impossible to ignore. States printed their own separate currencies. States taxed each other's goods at their borders, as if they were foreign countries trading with one another. Congress could not raise money to pay off Revolutionary War debts. It could not fund an army to put down unrest inside the country's own borders. One event made these weaknesses especially vivid. In 1786 and 1787, a Massachusetts farmer and former Revolutionary War soldier named Daniel Shays led an armed uprising of struggling farmers against high taxes and debt collection in the state. The national government under the Articles had no army of its own to help put the uprising down. Massachusetts had to rely on a privately funded militia instead. Shays's Rebellion frightened many leaders across the states, since it showed a government too weak even to keep basic order within its own borders. It became one of the clearest signs that the Articles of Confederation needed to be replaced, not just patched up. When you reach Week 6's study of the Constitutional Convention, you will see delegates arrive in Philadelphia. Most delegates did not come to fine-tune the Articles. They came, in most of their own minds, to replace the Articles outright. A government too weak to actually govern had turned out to be nearly as dangerous as a government with too much power.
