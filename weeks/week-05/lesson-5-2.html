📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Declaring independence was one problem. Building a government to replace what was thrown off turned out to be a much harder one.
Before Congress ever voted on independence, one pamphlet did more to move ordinary colonists toward that vote than any single speech in Congress. In January 1776, an English immigrant named Thomas Paine published Common Sense anonymously, and it sold more copies, relative to the colonial population, than any publication in American history before or since.

📚 PART 1 · THE PAMPHLET THAT CHANGED MINDS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Common Sense sold more copies, relative to the colonial population, than any American publication before or since. Before reading why it worked, what do you think made plain, forceful language more persuasive to ordinary readers than a formal political argument? (No wrong answer.)

Where earlier colonial writing had often argued narrowly about specific taxes or specific acts of Parliament, Paine attacked something bigger: the very idea of hereditary monarchy itself, and the assumption that the colonies owed permanent loyalty to a king across an ocean. Paine wrote in plain, forceful language ordinary readers could quote back, and by the time Congress took up Jefferson's draft five months later, Paine had already done much of the persuading.

📚 PART 2 · A GOVERNMENT DESIGNED TO BE WEAK

But declaring independence and building a functioning government were two separate problems, and the second proved far harder than the first. In November 1777, Congress adopted the Articles of Confederation as the new nation's first constitution, formally ratified by all thirteen states in 1781. Having just fought a war against what they saw as an overreaching central government, the framers of the Articles built a purposefully weak one: a confederation, a loose alliance of sovereign states, with a national Congress that had no power to tax, no power to regulate trade between the states, no national court system, no national executive to enforce its own laws, and a rule requiring the unanimous consent of all thirteen states to make any amendment at all.

📚 PART 3 · WHEN WEAKNESS BECOMES DANGEROUS

Within a few years, the consequences of this weakness became impossible to ignore. States printed their own currencies and taxed each other's goods at the borders. Congress could not raise money to pay Revolutionary War debts or fund an army to put down internal unrest. When you reach Week 6's Constitutional Convention, you will see delegates arrive in Philadelphia not to fine-tune the Articles, but, in most delegates' minds, to replace them outright, because a government too weak to govern turned out to be nearly as dangerous as one with too much power.
