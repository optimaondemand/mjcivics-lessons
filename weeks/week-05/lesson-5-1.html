📚 PART 1 · AN IRREVERSIBLE VOTE4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing Rousseau's vocabulary from Lesson 4.2. This week's reading introduces no new Subject Vocabulary terms.

general will
Rousseau's term for the collective judgment of the whole community about the common good, which every citizen has a genuine share in forming.
State, Sovereign, and Power
Rousseau's three names for the same “moral and collective body” formed by the social contract.

By the spring of 1776, the thirteen colonies had been at war with Great Britain for almost a year. But most delegates to the Second Continental Congress still hesitated to declare full independence. Years of harsh British policy had pushed opinion steadily toward separation. Britain had taxed the colonies without their consent. Britain had closed Boston's port. Britain had forced colonists to house British soldiers in their own homes. Britain had dismissed colonial assemblies that pushed back. All of this built up over years. But declaring independence outright was an act you could not take back. If the rebellion failed, every single signer would be considered a traitor in British eyes, and traitors could be executed.

📚 PART 2 · ADAMS STEPS BACK, JEFFERSON STEPS FORWARD4 min
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Adams pushed harder for independence than almost anyone in Congress, then insisted someone else write the document that would announce it. Before reading why, what is your first guess: practical strategy, personal humility, or something else? (No wrong answer.)

No one in Congress pushed harder for the vote than John Adams of Massachusetts. Adams was blunt and relentless. He had been arguing for independence since long before most of his colleagues were ready to hear it. In June 1776, Congress appointed a Committee of Five to draft a formal declaration explaining the colonies' reasons to the world. Adams sat on that committee. But he insisted that the actual writing fall to a quieter Virginian: Thomas Jefferson. Adams later explained his reasoning plainly. Jefferson wrote better than he did. And because Jefferson was a Virginian, his authorship would make the document harder to dismiss as just a New England complaint. This is one of the more remarkable acts of political humility in American history. The man who fought loudest for independence stepped back so someone else could give it its lasting words.

📚 PART 3 · A TWO-PART ARGUMENT4 min

Jefferson drew on ideas you already know well from Week 3: Locke's natural rights, and his theory of when a government loses its legitimacy. Jefferson built these ideas into a document with two distinct halves. The opening states a general theory of government's purpose. It explains the conditions under which people may rightfully change or get rid of that government. The much longer second half applies that theory to one specific case. It lists more than two dozen complaints against King George III. These complaints are meant to prove, point by point, that Britain's government had crossed exactly the line Locke described. This week's primary source is drawn from both halves of the document.

📚 PART 4 · THE VOTE, THE DATE, AND THE EDITS4 min

The vote for independence itself actually happened on July 2, 1776, when Congress formally approved separating from Britain. Two days later, on July 4, Congress approved the wording of Jefferson's document, the date we now celebrate as Independence Day. John Adams himself predicted that July 2 would be the date future Americans celebrated with fireworks and parades. He was only off by two days, since it was the document's approval, not the vote itself, that history ended up remembering most.

Jefferson's original draft also went through changes before Congress gave its final approval. Benjamin Franklin and John Adams, the other two most active members of the Committee of Five, reviewed and lightly edited Jefferson's draft before it ever reached the full Congress. Congress itself then debated the document for several days and cut about a fourth of Jefferson's original text. The most significant cut was a passage that had condemned the Atlantic slave trade and, by extension, the practice of slavery itself. Delegates from Georgia and South Carolina, along with some New England merchants involved in the slave trade, objected, and the passage was removed to preserve unity among the states. This is worth remembering. The document's stirring language about equality and unalienable rights survived. Its specific condemnation of slavery did not. You will return to this tension, between the Declaration's stated ideals and the nation's actual practices, later in this course.

One small edit shows how much these changes mattered. Jefferson's original draft said the truths in the Declaration were “sacred and undeniable.” Benjamin Franklin himself crossed out that phrase and wrote in “self-evident” instead. That is the very word you will read in this week's primary source. Franklin's edit was not just about style. “Sacred and undeniable” suggests something a religious authority insists on. “Self-evident” suggests something reason alone can prove true, without needing anyone's authority to back it up. This fits the Declaration's whole approach: appealing to reason and natural rights, not to religious or royal authority. Small edits like this one remind us that even a document as famous as the Declaration was a genuinely collaborative piece of writing, shaped by several sharp minds working together under enormous pressure and very little time. Keep that in mind as you read this week's primary source: you are reading the version Congress approved, the version history remembers, but not the only version that was ever written. Reading it that way, as a real historical artifact shaped by debate and compromise rather than as a single flash of inspiration, makes it easier to understand why its words still carry the weight they do.
