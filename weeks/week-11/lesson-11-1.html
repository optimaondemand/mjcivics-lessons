📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did the framers leave the judiciary's details so sparse, and what makes this branch dangerous in a different way than the other two?
If Article I is a detailed rulebook and Article II a careful list of powers, Article III is comparatively bare. It creates “one supreme Court,” and lets Congress decide whether to create any lower federal courts at all.

2

3
I will be able to explain what Hamilton meant by calling the judiciary “the least dangerous” branch.

📚 PART 1 · A BARE OUTLINE, FILLED IN LATER
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: do you think the Constitution required Congress to build a full system of federal courts right away, or left that decision open? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Congress did create lower federal courts, immediately, in the Judiciary Act of 1789, but the Constitution itself did not require it. The framers, having just spent most of their energy on the legislature and the executive, left the judiciary's finer details to be worked out later, by statute and by practice.

📚 PART 2 · TWO PROTECTIONS, ONE PURPOSE

Federal judges hold their offices “during good Behaviour,” in practice, for life, removable only through impeachment, and their salaries cannot be reduced while they serve. Both protections exist for the same reason: a judge who feared losing his job or his pay for an unpopular ruling would not be a genuinely independent check on the other branches.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Article III, Section 2 defines the kinds of cases federal courts may hear at all: cases arising under the Constitution, federal law, or treaties, along with disputes between states, or between citizens of different states. Federal courts cannot simply decide to rule on any dispute that interests them; their jurisdiction, like Congress's powers, is limited to what the text grants.
📚 PART 3 · “THE LEAST DANGEROUS” BRANCH

This is the branch Hamilton would go on to call, in this week's primary source, “the least dangerous” of the three, not because its work matters least, but because it commands neither the power of the purse Congress holds nor the power of the sword the President commands. What it has instead is judgment: the authority to say, in a case actually before it, what the law requires.
