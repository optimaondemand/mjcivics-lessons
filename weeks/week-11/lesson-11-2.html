📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If the Constitution never says courts can strike down a law, where does that power actually come from?
Here is the branch's strangest fact: nowhere does Article III explicitly say that federal courts may strike down a law as unconstitutional. That power, called judicial review, would not be formally exercised by the Supreme Court until 1803, in Marbury v. Madison. But fifteen years earlier, arguing for ratification, Alexander Hamilton had already predicted and defended exactly this power in Federalist No. 78.

📚 PART 1 · A POWER THE TEXT NEVER NAMES5 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 11.1, plus this week's key term.

“case or controversy” requirement
the rule that federal courts may only decide real disputes between real parties who have actually been harmed.
diversity jurisdiction
letting a lawsuit between citizens of different states be heard in a neutral federal court instead of a state court that might favor its own resident.
judicial review
the power of federal courts to strike down a law as unconstitutional, first exercised in 1803's Marbury v. Madison.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: does it surprise you that judicial review, one of the most famous powers of the Supreme Court, is not written explicitly anywhere in Article III? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

The case began in the final hours of the outgoing Adams administration in early 1801, when Adams and his outgoing Federalist Congress rushed to fill dozens of minor judicial posts before Thomas Jefferson took office. William Marbury was one of these last-minute appointees, named a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia. His commission was signed and sealed, but the outgoing administration ran out of time to actually deliver it before Jefferson's inauguration. Jefferson's new Secretary of State, James Madison, then simply refused to hand it over, since Jefferson considered the whole batch of eleventh-hour appointments improper. Marbury sued directly, asking the Supreme Court to order Madison to deliver his commission.

Chief Justice John Marshall's opinion in that case is where the Supreme Court first actually struck down an act of Congress as unconstitutional. The specific outcome is almost more interesting than the general principle it established: Marshall ruled that Marbury did have a right to his judicial commission, and that the executive branch had wrongly withheld it, but also ruled that the specific law Marbury relied on to bring his case directly to the Supreme Court was itself unconstitutional, since it expanded the Court's original jurisdiction beyond what Article III actually allows. The result was that Marbury lost his case, even though Marshall agreed he had been wronged, while the Supreme Court gained something far larger: an accepted power to declare an act of Congress void. Fifteen years earlier, Hamilton had already predicted and defended exactly this power, reasoning from the structure of the Constitution itself rather than from any single clause that names the power outright.

Marshall himself is worth remembering. He served as Chief Justice for 34 years, longer than almost any other Justice in American history, and used that unusually long tenure to shape how the Court thought about its own authority well beyond this single case. It is a small irony of the story that Marshall's own cousin, Thomas Jefferson, was the President whose administration he ruled against, a reminder that the framers' insistence on judicial independence was meant to hold even when a judge's own family ties ran directly into the case before him.

📚 PART 2 · HAMILTON'S CHAIN OF REASONING4 min

Hamilton's argument runs like this: the Constitution is a limited grant of authority from the people to their government. If Congress passes a law that exceeds the authority the Constitution actually grants it, that law conflicts with the higher law the people themselves adopted. Someone has to decide which document controls when they conflict, and Hamilton argued it must be the courts, whose entire job is interpreting law in the cases before them, not because judges are wiser than legislators, but because “the interpretation of the laws is the proper and peculiar province of the courts,” and a court applying the Constitution over a conflicting statute is not overruling the people, but enforcing the people's own prior decision against their temporary representatives.

Hamilton pushed the comparison further. Representatives, he argued, are servants of the people, not their masters, so it would be strange indeed if a servant's ordinary acts could override the master's own founding instructions. Courts applying the Constitution over a conflicting statute were not defying popular will in his account, but enforcing the one act of popular will that outranks every ordinary law: the Constitution itself, adopted directly by the people rather than passed by their temporary representatives in a single session of Congress.

📚 PART 3 · WHY IT DOESN'T BECOME A ROVING COMMISSION4 min

This argument depends entirely on everything Article III already establishes: life tenure insulates judges from the political pressure that might otherwise tempt them to rubber-stamp whatever the current Congress wants, and the limited, defined jurisdiction of Section 2 means judges exercise this power only inside real cases, never as a roving commission to review laws in the abstract. Hamilton's “least dangerous branch” turns out to hold one of the most consequential powers of all, consequential precisely because it is exercised so narrowly.

Congress does retain one structural check on this power. Article III lets Congress set exceptions to the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction, meaning Congress can, within limits, remove certain categories of cases from the Court's reach altogether. This tool has been used only rarely across American history, but it is a reminder that even judicial reviewthe power of federal courts to strike down a law as unconstitutional, first exercised in 1803's Marbury v. Madison. operates inside boundaries the other two branches still help define.

It is also worth noticing that this is a specifically American choice, not an inevitable feature of every constitutional government. Other democracies built different answers to the same problem: some assign constitutional review to a specialized constitutional court kept separate from ordinary judges, and some, including the United Kingdom for most of its history, give Parliament itself the final word on what a law means, with no court empowered to override it. The United States is unusual in handing this power to ordinary courts deciding ordinary cases, an arrangement Hamilton defended before it ever existed and Marshall's Court claimed for the first time fifteen years later.

⚡ FLIP IT · TRACE HAMILTON'S ARGUMENT
Congress passes a law that exceeds the authority the Constitution grants it. What happens next, in Hamilton's argument?
That law conflicts with the Constitution, the people's own higher law, so a court must treat it as invalid rather than enforce it.
⚡ FLIP IT · TRACE HAMILTON'S ARGUMENT
Why should courts, rather than Congress itself, decide whether a law is constitutional?
Because interpreting law is, in Hamilton's words, the courts' proper and peculiar province, not a job legislators are suited to do for their own laws.
⚡ FLIP IT · TRACE HAMILTON'S ARGUMENT
Why does life tenure matter for this power to work as Hamilton intended?
Judges insulated from political pressure are less tempted to simply approve whatever the current Congress wants, which is what makes their judgment independent.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · JUDGMENT, NOT AN ANSWER KEY
AI tools can summarize a case or lay out a legal argument's steps quickly. It cannot exercise the kind of judgment Hamilton insisted courts alone must supply: weighing one specific law against the Constitution inside a real case, not producing the fastest plausible answer.
