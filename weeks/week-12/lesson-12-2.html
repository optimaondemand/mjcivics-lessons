📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single sentence, argued over for two centuries: what does the Second Amendment actually protect?
No amendment in the Bill of Rights has generated more sustained argument over its exact meaning than the Second. Its single sentence contains a stated justification and a stated right, and the relationship between the two is precisely what courts have spent over two centuries working out.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the two competing interpretations of the Second Amendment: an individual right versus a right tied to militia service.
2
I will be able to explain the historical context, especially the English Bill of Rights of 1689, that shaped the framers' concern about a disarmed population and a standing army.
3
I will be able to describe how District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) resolved the individual-versus-militia debate.

📚 PART 1 · ONE SENTENCE, TWO READINGS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read Part 2 and 3: based on the amendment's text alone, "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed," which reading feels more natural to you? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Does the amendment protect an individual's right to own weapons for personal use, or does it protect that right only in connection with organized militia service? The stated justification, "a well regulated Militia," and the stated right, "the right of the people to keep and bear Arms," sit side by side, and readers have disagreed for generations about how tightly the first clause limits the second.

📚 PART 2 · WHY THE FRAMERS CARED

Context from Week 5's Paine reading helps explain why the framers cared about this question at all. The Revolution had been fought substantially by citizen militias, not a professional standing army, and English political tradition, reaching back to the 1689 English Bill of Rights, which listed a standing army kept "without Consent of Parlyament" among its grievances against James II, treated a disarmed population and an unaccountable standing army as classic tools of tyranny. A right to bear arms, in this reading, was inseparable from the practical ability of ordinary citizens to resist a government that betrayed the social contract Locke and Rousseau had described.

📚 PART 3 · HELLER SETTLES THE QUESTION, FOR NOW

The Supreme Court did not resolve the individual-versus-militia question definitively until District of Columbia v. Heller in 2008, which held the amendment protects an individual right to keep a firearm for lawful purposes such as self-defense, unconnected with militia service. This is a reminder that even amendments ratified in 1791 can remain live constitutional questions well into the twenty-first century, decided not by rewriting the text but by judges applying it, exactly as Hamilton described in last week's Federalist No. 78. A right that is not clearly infringed upon still had to wait over two centuries for a definitive Supreme Court ruling on what it actually covers.
