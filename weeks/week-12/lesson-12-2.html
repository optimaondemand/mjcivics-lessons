📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A single sentence, argued over for two centuries: what does the Second Amendment actually protect?
No amendment in the Bill of Rights has generated more sustained argument over its exact meaning than the Second. Its single sentence contains a stated justification and a stated right, and the relationship between the two is precisely what courts have spent over two centuries working out.

3
I will be able to describe how District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) resolved the individual-versus-militia debate.

📚 PART 1 · ONE SENTENCE, TWO READINGS4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 12.1, plus one new term for this week.

Bill of Rights
the first ten amendments to the Constitution, ratified in 1791, restraining what the federal government may do to individual rights.
incorporation (in-kor-por-AY-shun)
the gradual process of applying most Bill of Rights protections against state governments too.
militia (muh-LISH-uh)
a body of ordinary citizens, trained and organized rather than professional soldiers, that the Second Amendment names as “necessary to the security of a free State.”

Does the amendment protect an individual's right to own weapons for personal use, or does it protect that right only in connection with organized militia service? The stated justification, "a well regulated Militia," and the stated right, "the right of the people to keep and bear Arms," sit side by side, and readers have disagreed for generations about how tightly the first clause limits the second.

Part of the debate turns on how language has shifted since 1791. In eighteenth-century usage, "well regulated" meant well trained, well disciplined, and properly organized and equipped, not "restricted by government rules" in the sense the word "regulated" often carries today. A "well regulated Militia," in the framers' own vocabulary, meant a militia that was actually prepared and organized for service, a description of military readiness rather than of legal control.

📚 PART 2 · WHY THE FRAMERS CARED4 min

Context from Week 5's Paine reading helps explain why the framers cared about this question at all. The Revolution had been fought substantially by citizen militias, not a professional standing army, and English political tradition, reaching back to the 1689 English Bill of Rights, which listed a standing army kept "without Consent of Parlyament" among its grievances against James II, treated a disarmed population and an unaccountable standing army as classic tools of tyranny. A right to bear arms, in this reading, was inseparable from the practical ability of ordinary citizens to resist a government that betrayed the social contract Locke and Rousseau had described.

Congress gave this idea legal shape almost immediately. The Militia Act of 1792 defined the militia(muh-LISH-uh)a body of ordinary citizens, trained and organized rather than professional soldiers, that the Second Amendment names as “necessary to the security of a free State.” broadly, enrolling virtually every able-bodied male citizen between eighteen and forty-five and requiring each to arm himself at his own expense. Under this understanding, "the militia" was not a small, separately selected group; it was, in principle, the whole body of eligible citizens. This is part of why some readers of the amendment see the individual right and the militia purpose as two sides of the same coin, rather than as separate, competing claims.

📚 PART 3 · HELLER SETTLES THE QUESTION, FOR NOW6 min

The Supreme Court did not resolve the individual-versus-militia question definitively until District of Columbia v. Heller in 2008, which held the amendment protects an individual right to keep a firearm for lawful purposes such as self-defense, unconnected with militia service. This is a reminder that even amendments ratified in 1791 can remain live constitutional questions well into the twenty-first century, decided not by rewriting the text but by judges applying it, exactly as Hamilton described in last week's Federalist No. 78. A right that is not clearly infringed upon still had to wait over two centuries for a definitive Supreme Court ruling on what it actually covers.

It is worth knowing that Heller did not eliminate all gun regulation. The Court's opinion explicitly stated that the right is not unlimited, and that many longstanding regulations, such as bans on possession by felons or restrictions in sensitive places like schools and government buildings, remain presumptively lawful. Two years later, in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010), the Court applied the Second Amendment against state and local governments too, using the same incorporation process from Lesson 12.1. Before McDonald, a state or city government was not directly bound by the Second Amendment at all. Together, Heller and McDonald show incorporation working in two separate steps: first the Court decides what a right actually means, then a later case decides whether that meaning binds state and local governments too, not just the federal government the amendment's framers were originally most worried about.

The case itself began with Dick Heller, a licensed special police officer in Washington, D.C., who was permitted to carry a handgun on duty but was barred by district law from keeping one at his own home for self-defense. Heller sued, arguing the district's near-total ban on functional handguns violated his Second Amendment rights. The Supreme Court agreed, striking down the D.C. law by a narrow five-to-four vote, a margin worth noting: even after two centuries of the amendment sitting in the Constitution, the justices closest to actually deciding its meaning were sharply divided.

It is easy to assume the Supreme Court settles a constitutional question as soon as it comes up, but the Second Amendment shows how misleading that assumption can be. Between 1791 and 2008, the Court decided only a small handful of cases even touching the amendment's core meaning, and none resolved the individual-versus-militia debate directly. Many state constitutions, including Florida's, contain their own separate right-to-bear-arms language, often written in more explicitly individual terms than the federal amendment. This meant, for over two centuries, that the practical scope of gun rights in any given state often depended more on that state's own constitution and courts than on the federal Second Amendment, federalism at work again, the same layered system of national and state authority you have traced since Week 7.
