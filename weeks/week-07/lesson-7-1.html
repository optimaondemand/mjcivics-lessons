📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Separating power on paper is easy. What actually keeps each branch in its own lane once the government is running?
The Constitution the Philadelphia delegates signed in September 1787 was not automatically the law of the land. It still had to be ratified by conventions in at least nine of the thirteen states, and ratification was anything but guaranteed. Critics calling themselves Anti-Federalists warned that the new government was too strong, too distant from ordinary citizens, and dangerously light on explicit protections for individual rights, a fear that would eventually produce the Bill of Rights you will study in Module 4. Supporters, the Federalists, needed to answer these objections in public and in detail.

What shared pen name did Hamilton, Madison, and Jay publish the Federalist Papers under?
"Publius." Between October 1787 and August 1788, they published 85 essays arguing state by state, clause by clause, for ratification.

Between October 1787 and August 1788, Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay published 85 essays under the shared pen name "Publius," collected today as The Federalist Papers, arguing state by state, clause by clause, for ratification. Madison's contributions include some of the most searching defenses of the Constitution's structure ever written, and none is more famous than Federalist No. 51, which tackles the separation-of-powers problem you studied through Montesquieu in Week 4 and asks a harder follow-up question: separating power on paper is easy, but what actually keeps each branch in its own lane once the government is running?

📚 PART 2 · AMBITION MUST COUNTERACT AMBITION

Madison's answer rejected the comfortable assumption that good character alone would hold the system together. Instead, he argued the Constitution should give each branch the structural means and the personal motive to resist encroachment by the others, famously summarized in his phrase "ambition must be made to counteract ambition." This is not cynicism for its own sake; it is a design principle. Rather than hoping officeholders will be virtuous, build a system where even self-interested officeholders, each jealously guarding their own branch's power, end up protecting the separation of powers as a side effect.
