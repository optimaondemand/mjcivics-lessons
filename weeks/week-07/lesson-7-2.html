📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens when a single persuasive speaker and a wave of popular enthusiasm meet no structural delay at all?
Madison and his contemporaries were classically educated men who read Greek and Roman history not as dusty trivia but as a working archive of political case studies. One episode looms especially large behind the framers' distrust of unmediated, direct democratic decision-making: the Athenian Assembly's vote, in 415 BC, to launch a massive invasion of Sicily during the middle of the ongoing Peloponnesian War against Sparta.

📚 PART 1 · A VOTE SWEPT UP IN ENTHUSIASM

The historian Thucydides, in his History of the Peloponnesian War, recorded how the charismatic and ambitious Athenian general Alcibiades persuaded the Assembly to approve the expedition against the cautious warnings of the older statesman Nicias, who argued the campaign was reckless and poorly justified. The Assembly, swept up in the promise of glory and wealth, voted for the largest and most expensive military expedition Athens had ever launched, and then, in a further complication, recalled Alcibiades on corruption charges partway through the campaign, leaving the invasion in the hands of the very general who had warned against it.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read the outcome: given what you just read about the Assembly's mood, do you think this expedition succeeded or failed? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

The expedition ended in catastrophe: the entire Athenian fleet and army were destroyed or captured, a blow from which Athens never fully recovered.

📚 PART 2 · THE LESSON THE FRAMERS TOOK FROM IT

This is exactly the danger Madison and his fellow Federalists worried a "pure" democracy could not avoid: a single persuasive speaker, momentary popular enthusiasm, and no structural delay or check standing between an emotional majority and an irreversible decision. It is why the Constitution's design deliberately avoids direct popular votes on most matters, instead layering decisions through elected representatives, a bicameral Congress, and a separate executive: a federalism of shared and divided authority between the people, the states, and the national government, discussed further in the Federalist Papers you will read next week. Thucydides gave the framers a two-thousand-year-old case study in exactly what unchecked popular passion, unslowed by any structural delay, can do to a republic.
