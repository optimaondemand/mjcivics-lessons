📚 PART 1 · THREE AMENDMENTS, ONE GAP
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 13.2, plus new terms for the right to remain silent.

exclusionary rule
the rule that a confession obtained in violation of Miranda cannot normally be used as evidence against the suspect at trial.
Miranda warnings (muh-RAN-duh)
the specific warnings police must give a suspect who is in custody, before any interrogation begins.
Sherbert test
the rule that once a law substantially burdens someone's free exercise of religion, government must show a “compelling state interest.”
strict scrutiny
the highest level of legal review American courts use, requiring a “compelling state interest” to justify a law.

Three amendments work together to protect someone accused of a crime: the Fourth (against unreasonable searches and seizures), the Fifth (against being compelled to be "a witness against himself," among other protections), and the Sixth (guaranteeing "the Assistance of Counsel for his defence"). None of these amendments explains exactly how police must behave once they take a suspect into custody, and in 1966, that gap produced one of the most famous rulings in the Court's history.

Ernesto Miranda had confessed to a crime during police interrogation after being taken into custody, without ever being told he had a right to remain silent or a right to a lawyer. In Miranda v. Arizona, Chief Justice Earl Warren's majority opinion held that the Fifth Amendment's protection against self-incrimination could not function as intended inside the pressure of custodial interrogation unless suspects were clearly informed of their rights beforehand. The Court did not simply strike down Miranda's conviction; it wrote out the specific warning police must give once a suspect is in custody and before any interrogation begins: the "Miranda warnings" that have since become a fixture of American police procedure and popular culture alike.

📚 PART 2 · CUSTODY, NOT JUST A QUESTION
⚡ TRY IT · DOES THIS SITUATION REQUIRE A MIRANDA WARNING?
It is worth being precise about when this warning actually applies, since popular culture often gets it wrong. For each situation below, decide whether a Miranda warning is legally required.
1. An officer asks a bystander at a car accident what happened
2. A suspect is formally arrested, taken to the station, and interrogated about the crime
3. A detective asks a few preliminary questions of someone who is free to walk away at any time

Miranda warnings are required only once a person is in police custody, either formally arrested or reasonably believing they are not free to leave, and only before questioning meant to draw out incriminating information. An officer asking a bystander what happened at a car accident, or a detective asking a few preliminary questions of someone who is free to walk away, needs no warning at all, because no one is in custody yet. The trigger is custody together with interrogation, not simply the fact that an officer is asking questions.

📚 PART 3 · MAKING A RIGHT ACTUALLY WORK
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Notice the structural move here, the same one you saw in Lemon v. Kurtzman last week: the Court is not adding new words to the Fifth or Sixth Amendments. It is building a specific, practical procedure that makes an existing constitutional guarantee actually effective in the real conditions of a police station, where an unrepresented suspect facing trained interrogators is in a far weaker position than the Constitution's text alone can protect.

It matters, too, what actually happens if police skip these warnings. A confession obtained in violation of Miranda cannot normally be used as evidence against the suspect at trial, a consequence called the exclusionary rule. Some critics have argued this lets obviously guilty people escape punishment on a technicality; supporters respond that the only way to make the Fifth Amendment's protection actually mean something is to remove the government's incentive to violate it in the first place. A right with no real consequence for its violation is barely a right at all.

Ernesto Miranda's own story did not end with the Supreme Court's ruling. Arizona retried him without using his original confession as evidence, and prosecutors convicted him anyway based on other evidence, including testimony from his girlfriend. After his release from prison years later, Miranda sometimes made money autographing police-issued Miranda warning cards. He was killed in a bar fight in 1976, and in a notable irony, the suspect arrested in connection with his death was read his Miranda rights and chose to remain silent; no one was ever convicted of Miranda's killing.

The promise that “if he cannot afford an attorney one will be appointed for him” did not come from thin air. Three years before Miranda, in Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), the Supreme Court had already ruled that the Sixth Amendment's right to counsel requires states to provide a free lawyer to any criminal defendant who cannot afford one, at least in serious cases. Clarence Earl Gideon, the defendant in that case, had been forced to represent himself in a Florida courtroom after a judge refused his request for a lawyer, and was convicted. Gideon's own handwritten appeal to the Supreme Court, written in pencil from his prison cell, helped establish a right that Miranda's warnings would later require police to actually announce out loud. Read together, Gideon and Miranda show the Sixth and Fifth Amendments reinforcing each other: one guarantees a lawyer's help, the other guarantees a suspect actually learns that help is available.
