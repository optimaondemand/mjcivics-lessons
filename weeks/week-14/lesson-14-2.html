📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Must every police encounter meet the full standard required for a formal arrest?
The Fourth Amendment protects against "unreasonable searches and seizures," and ordinarily requires probable cause and, for a search of a home, a warrant. But police work often happens on the street, in the seconds before a crime occurs rather than after, and Terry v. Ohio (1968) asked whether the full protections of a formal arrest and search must apply to a much briefer, more limited police encounter.

📚 PART 1 · A PATTERN THE DETECTIVE RECOGNIZED4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing two terms from Lesson 14.1, plus new terms for street-level policing.

exclusionary rule
the rule that a confession obtained in violation of Miranda cannot normally be used as evidence at trial.
Miranda warnings (muh-RAN-duh)
the specific warnings police must give a suspect who is in custody, before any interrogation begins.
probable cause
enough facts that a reasonable person would believe a crime has probably been committed; required for a full arrest or search warrant.
reasonable suspicion
specific facts suggesting criminal activity might be happening; a lower standard than probable cause, enough only for a brief stop and pat-down.
Terry stop
a brief investigative stop and pat-down for weapons, justified by reasonable suspicion rather than probable cause.

Detective Martin McFadden had watched John Terry and two companions repeatedly walk past and peer into a store window in a pattern he recognized, from years of experience, as the behavior of men casing a location for a robbery. McFadden approached, identified himself, and patted down the outside of Terry's clothing, discovering a concealed weapon. Terry argued this "stop and frisk" was an unconstitutional search conducted without probable causeenough facts that a reasonable person would believe a crime has probably been committed; required for a full arrest or search warrant..

McFadden watched the three men from a distance for roughly ten to twelve minutes, during which each man walked to the same store window, peered inside, walked on, conferred with the others, and repeated the sequence about a dozen times combined. This was not a single passing glance; it was a deliberate, repeated pattern McFadden recognized specifically because of his own decades of experience patrolling that same downtown Cleveland beat. He approached only after this extended observation, not on a first impression, which mattered directly to how the Court would later evaluate whether his suspicion was genuinely reasonable rather than a mere hunch.

⚡ FLIP CARD · CLICK TO REVEAL
Did the Supreme Court agree that this search violated the Fourth Amendment?
✅ ANSWER
No. The Supreme Court disagreed, holding that an officer may briefly stop and pat down a person for weapons based on "reasonable suspicion," a lower standard than probable cause, when the officer can point to "specific and articulable facts" suggesting danger, provided the search stays limited to discovering weapons rather than becoming a general search for evidence.
📚 PART 2 · A SLIDING SCALE, NOT AN ON-OFF SWITCH7 min
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Read alongside Miranda, Terry shows the Fourth, Fifth, and Sixth Amendments operating on a sliding scale rather than as fixed on-off switches: a brief stop requires less justification than a full arrest, just as an informal, non-custodial conversation requires no Miranda warning while a custodial interrogation does. The Court's task, case after case, is calibrating exactly how much government intrusion is "reasonable" at each point along that scale, the same kind of line-drawing you saw the religion clauses require in Week 13.

It helps to see the two standards side by side. Probable cause requires enough facts that a reasonable person would believe a crime has probably been committed by the person in question; it justifies a full arrest or a search warrant. Reasonable suspicionspecific facts suggesting criminal activity might be happening; a lower standard than probable cause, enough only for a brief stop and pat-down. requires less: specific facts suggesting criminal activity might be happening, enough to justify a brief stop and a pat-down for weapons, but not enough to justify a full arrest or a search of pockets and bags for evidence. An officer who wants to escalate from a Terry stopa brief investigative stop and pat-down for weapons, justified by reasonable suspicion rather than probable cause. to a full arrest needs to develop probable cause first, usually by observing something during the stop itself, like a visible weapon or an admission.

The Fourth Amendment's own history explains why "reasonableness" became the operative word at all. Colonial Americans deeply resented British writs of assistance, general search warrants that let officials search almost any home or ship for smuggled goods without specifying what they were looking for or why. The framers wrote the Fourth Amendment's requirement of a warrant "particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized" directly in response to that grievance. Terry's brief pat-down looks nothing like a general writ of assistance, precisely because reasonable suspicion, unlike those old general warrants, requires specific, individualized, articulable facts about a particular person in a particular moment, not a blanket license to search whatever an official finds convenient.

Terry's reasonable-suspicion standard has remained controversial in practice, even though the legal rule itself is well settled. Critics have argued that large-scale stop-and-frisk programs, especially one run for years by the New York City Police Department, were applied in ways that disproportionately targeted young men in minority neighborhoods, often without genuinely individualized suspicion for each stop. A federal court found in Floyd v. City of New York (2013) that the city's specific program, as actually practiced, violated the Fourth Amendment and the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection guarantee you will read about next week. A legal standard can be constitutionally sound in theory while still being misapplied, in ways courts can later correct, in actual practice.

Justice William O. Douglas was the lone dissenter in Terry v. Ohio. He worried that allowing searches based on suspicion lower than probable cause, even for a brief pat-down, handed police a new power the Fourth Amendment's text did not clearly authorize, and predicted the ruling would be read more broadly over time than the Court initially intended. Since 1968, the term “Terry stop” has indeed become a general legal term covering brief investigative stops well beyond the specific pedestrian encounter the original case involved, including brief stops of vehicles during traffic encounters. Douglas's dissent is worth remembering: even a ruling that seems settled and uncontroversial today was often decided by a divided Court, with genuinely serious arguments on more than one side.

Terry v. Ohio remains one of the most cited Fourth Amendment cases in American law, cited not only in later stop-and-frisk disputes but in cases about brief traffic stops, airport security screening, and certain school searches. Courts still use its core question today: given the specific facts an officer can point to, was a brief, limited intrusion reasonable under the circumstances? That single question has had to stretch across an enormous range of situations the Court in 1968 could not have specifically anticipated when it first wrote the opinion.
