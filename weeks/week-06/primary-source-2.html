Machiavelli, Discourses on Livy, Book I, Ch. 4
Read this, then complete 2.41 Primary Source Exercise in Canvas.
📖 CONTEXT

The Italian Renaissance writer Niccolò Machiavelli's Discourses on Livy (1531) revived and extended Polybius's argument about Rome's mixed constitution for a Renaissance audience. Here Machiavelli goes further than Polybius, arguing that open conflict between Rome's nobles and its common people was not a defect but the very engine that produced laws favorable to liberty. Read it alongside Lesson 6.2's account of how this idea reached James Madison.

📜 THE TEXT
“To me those who condemn the quarrels between the nobles and the plebs, seem to be cavilling at the very things that were the primary cause of Rome’s retaining her freedom… in every republic there are two parties, that of the nobles and that of the people; and… all the laws that are made in favor of liberty result from the disunion of these parties… nor can it in reason be otherwise, for as the object of the nobles is to command, and that of the people not to be commanded, it is impossible but that these tumults, and all the other evils that may arise from them, must promote rather than diminish liberty, in as much as they keep alive that jealousy of encroachment which is the great safeguard of free institutions.”
(Standard public-domain translation, condensed.)
