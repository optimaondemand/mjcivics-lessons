Philadelphia, Summer 1787: Writing a New Frame of Government

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Congress sent delegates to Philadelphia to fix the Articles of Confederation. Why did most of them decide to write something entirely new instead?
Week 5 left the new United States governed by the Articles of Confederation: a Congress with no power to tax, no power to regulate trade, no national court, no national executive, and a rule requiring all thirteen states to agree before anything about the system could change. In May 1787, delegates from twelve states (Rhode Island refused to send anyone) gathered in Philadelphia with instructions from Congress to revise the Articles. Within days, most delegates had abandoned that instruction entirely and begun drafting something new.

📚 PART 1 · TWO PLANS, ONE DEADLOCK
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read on: the delegates were only instructed to revise the Articles of Confederation. Do you think most of them stuck to that instruction, or went further? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

The Virginia Plan and the New Jersey Plan

The Convention's first real fight was over representation. The Virginia Plan, largely shaped by James Madison before the Convention even opened, proposed a strong national government with a legislature apportioned by state population, a plan that favored large states like Virginia and Pennsylvania. Smaller states countered with the New Jersey Plan, which kept the Confederation's equal representation for every state regardless of size. The deadlock threatened to end the Convention before it produced anything.

The Great Compromise

The solution, worked out through weeks of committee negotiation and known ever since as the Great Compromise (or Connecticut Compromise), created the two-chamber Congress you will study in detail in Module 3: a bicameral legislature with a House of Representatives apportioned by population, and a Senate with equal representation for every state.

📚 PART 2 · THE PREAMBLE: A POSITIVE STATEMENT OF PURPOSE

By September, the delegates had produced a complete plan of government, opening with a single sentence explaining what the whole document was for before laying out a single power or office. This week's first primary source is that opening, the Preamble, and it is worth reading slowly, because unlike the Declaration's list of grievances against a king, the Preamble is a positive statement of purpose: what this new government exists to do, for whom, and why. Notice, too, that the document is meant to secure liberty's blessings not only for the people living in 1787, but for their posterity, the generations still to come.
