📚 PART 1 · ONE EXECUTIVE, NOT A COUNCIL4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing three terms from Lesson 9.2, plus new terms for the presidency.

Commander in Chief (kuh-MAN-der in CHEEF)
the President's constitutional role as head of the armed forces, though only Congress can declare war.
enumerated powers (ee-NOO-mer-ay-ted POW-erz)
the doctrine that a national legislature may only exercise the powers specifically named for it in the text.
“high Crimes and Misdemeanors”
the Constitution's phrase for the grounds for impeachment; unlike treason or bribery, it has no single fixed legal definition.
Necessary and Proper Clause
the clause letting Congress make laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers; critics call it the “elastic clause.”
pardon (PAR-dun)
the President's power to forgive a federal crime or delay its punishment.
police power
the broad power to regulate for public health, safety, and morals, left to the states.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: recall Rome's two consuls from Week 2. Do you think a single President is a safer design than two officers who check each other, or a riskier one? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Article II creates something the Articles of Confederation had deliberately refused to create: a single national executive. Recall from Week 2 that Rome's answer to the danger of one-man rule was a dual executive, two consuls who could check each other. The Constitutional Convention debated this question seriously, and chose one President instead. The delegates judged that a single officer could act with a speed and accountability that a council could not match, as long as his powers were narrowly listed and checked by the other branches.

This choice was not obvious or unanimous at the Convention. Edmund Randolph of Virginia, who had introduced the Virginia Plan back in Week 6, argued strongly for a plural executive, a small council of three or more people sharing power together, precisely to avoid recreating anything resembling a king. Other delegates, including James Wilson of Pennsylvania, argued just as strongly for a single executive, believing a council would blur responsibility and make it harder for citizens to know exactly whom to praise or blame for the executive branch's actions. Alexander Hamilton, whose Federalist essays you will read later this week, went even further than Wilson during the Convention's private debates: he floated an executive serving on good behavior for life, closer to a king than almost any other delegate was willing to accept. The Convention rejected that idea quickly, but Hamilton's willingness to argue for it shows how wide a range of executive designs the delegates actually considered before settling on a single President serving a fixed term. The single-executive side eventually won the debate, but the worry behind Randolph's argument, that concentrating executive power in one person courts danger, is exactly what the rest of Article II's careful limits are built to answer.

📚 PART 2 · A SHORT LIST OF NAMED POWERS4 min

Article II, Sections 2 and 3 read almost like Article I, Section 8 in miniature. They give a specific list of granted powers, not a general grant of authority. The President is Commander in Chief(kuh-MAN-der in CHEEF)the President's constitutional role as head of the armed forces, though only Congress can declare war. of the armed forces, but only Congress can declare war. He may grant pardons(PAR-dun)the President's power to forgive a federal crime or delay its punishment. and reprieves, meaning he can forgive a federal crime or delay its punishment. He may make treaties and appoint ambassadors, judges, and other federal officers, but only “by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate.” That is the very power you studied last week as one of the Senate's distinguishing functions. Section 3 adds two smaller but real duties: the President must periodically give Congress information on “the State of the Union,” and he alone receives foreign ambassadors, which is part of why the President, not Congress, generally speaks for the country in dealings with other nations. And he is charged to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.” This is a duty to enforce the law, not to write it.

📚 PART 3 · POWERS BUILT TO NEED THE OTHER BRANCHES4 min

Every one of these powers assumes another branch standing nearby, ready to participate or object. A President cannot make a binding treaty alone; he needs two-thirds of the Senate. He cannot seat a federal judge alone; he needs a Senate majority to confirm. The single executive the framers created is powerful, but it is not an executive that acts by itself; it is an executive built to need the other branches, by design.

⚡ FLIP IT · WHAT DOES THIS POWER ACTUALLY REQUIRE?
Makes treaties with foreign nations
Requires two-thirds of the Senate present to concur before the treaty takes effect. Not a power the President can exercise alone.
⚡ FLIP IT · WHAT DOES THIS POWER ACTUALLY REQUIRE?
Appoints ambassadors, judges, and other federal officers
Requires the Senate's Advice and Consent, confirmed by a majority vote. The President nominates; the Senate decides whether the nomination stands.
⚡ FLIP IT · WHAT DOES THIS POWER ACTUALLY REQUIRE?
Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy
Requires nothing from Congress to hold the title, but only Congress can declare war in the first place. Command and the decision to go to war are split between the branches.
📚 PART 4 · TERMS, SUCCESSION, AND IMPEACHMENT4 min

The original Constitution set no limit on how many terms a President could serve, only a four-year term length. George Washington voluntarily stepped down after two terms, and this two-term tradition held as an unwritten norm for nearly a century and a half. Franklin Roosevelt broke that tradition during the Great Depression and World War II, winning four consecutive elections. In response, the 22nd Amendment, ratified in 1951, wrote the two-term limit directly into the Constitution's text, turning Washington's voluntary example into an enforceable rule.

Article II also creates the Vice Presidency, whose primary constitutional job is remarkably narrow: wait to succeed the President if the President dies, resigns, or becomes unable to serve. For most of American history, the exact rules for handling presidential disability were vague and untested. The 25th Amendment, ratified in 1967, finally spelled out a clear process for succession and for temporarily transferring power if a President becomes incapacitated, closing a gap the original Constitution had simply left open.

Article II, Section 4 also states the specific grounds for removing a President, Vice President, or other civil officer through impeachment: “Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanorsthe Constitution's phrase for the grounds for impeachment; unlike treason or bribery, it has no single fixed legal definition..” The first two terms are fairly specific. The third, “high Crimes and Misdemeanors,” has never been given one single fixed legal definition, and Congress has had to work out its meaning case by case across American history. This ambiguity connects directly to last week's account of the Senate's role as the body that actually tries these cases. Notice, too, that impeachment itself is a shared power split across two branches: the House alone decides whether to bring charges, and the Senate alone decides guilt, exactly the kind of divided authority this whole course keeps returning to.

In practice, the two-branch design has made removal extremely rare. Andrew Johnson became the first President impeached by the House, in 1868, over his conflict with Congress during Reconstruction; the Senate fell one vote short of the two-thirds needed to convict him. More than a century passed before it happened again. No President has ever actually been removed from office through this process, a fact that says less about how well presidents have behaved and more about how high the Constitution deliberately set the bar for using impeachment against the nation's chief executive.
