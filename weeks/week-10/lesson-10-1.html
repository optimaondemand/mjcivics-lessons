📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did the Convention choose one President instead of a council, and what did it refuse to give him in return?
Welcome to Quarter 2. Last quarter closed inside Article I, tracing how Congress is structured and what it may do. This quarter opens with Article II, the shortest of the three branch articles, which creates something the Articles of Confederation had deliberately refused to create: a single national executive.

I will be able to explain why the Convention chose a single President instead of Rome's dual-executive model of two consuls.
2
I will be able to list the specific powers Article II, §§2–3 grants the President.
3
I will be able to explain why nearly every one of these powers requires cooperation from Congress or the Senate.

📚 PART 1 · ONE EXECUTIVE, NOT A COUNCIL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: recall Rome's two consuls from Week 2. Do you think a single President is a safer design than two officers who check each other, or a riskier one? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Recall from Week 2 that Rome's answer to the danger of one-man rule was a dual executive: two consuls who could check each other. The Constitutional Convention, after real debate, chose one President instead, judging that a single officer could act with the speed and accountability a council could not, provided his powers were narrowly listed and checked by the other branches.

📚 PART 2 · A SHORT LIST OF NAMED POWERS

Article II, Sections 2 and 3 read almost like Article I, Section 8 in miniature: a specific list of granted powers, not a general grant of authority. The President is Commander in Chief of the armed forces, but only Congress can declare war. He may grant pardons and reprieves. He may make treaties and appoint ambassadors, judges, and other federal officers, but only “by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate,” the very power you studied last week as one of the Senate's distinguishing functions. And he is charged to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed,” a duty to enforce, not to write, the law.

📚 PART 3 · POWERS BUILT TO NEED THE OTHER BRANCHES

Every one of these powers assumes another branch standing nearby, ready to participate or object. A President cannot make a binding treaty alone; he needs two-thirds of the Senate. He cannot seat a federal judge alone; he needs a Senate majority to confirm. The single executive the framers created is powerful, but it is not an executive that acts by itself; it is an executive built to need the other branches, by design.

⚡ FLIP IT · WHAT DOES THIS POWER ACTUALLY REQUIRE?
Makes treaties with foreign nations
Requires two-thirds of the Senate present to concur before the treaty takes effect. Not a power the President can exercise alone.
⚡ FLIP IT · WHAT DOES THIS POWER ACTUALLY REQUIRE?
Appoints ambassadors, judges, and other federal officers
Requires the Senate's Advice and Consent, confirmed by a majority vote. The President nominates; the Senate decides whether the nomination stands.
⚡ FLIP IT · WHAT DOES THIS POWER ACTUALLY REQUIRE?
Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy
Requires nothing from Congress to hold the title, but only Congress can declare war in the first place. Command and the decision to go to war are split between the branches.
