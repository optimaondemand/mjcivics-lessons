📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Where is the veto actually written into the Constitution, and why did Hamilton think the President needed it?
Here is a fact that surprises most students: the presidential veto is not found anywhere in Article II. It is written into Article I, Section 7, the section describing how a bill becomes law, a reminder that the Constitution's separation of powers is not three sealed compartments but one continuous process the branches pass back and forth.

📚 PART 1 · THE VETO'S ACTUAL ADDRESS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: where would you guess the veto power is written in the Constitution, Article I or Article II? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

Once both chambers of Congress pass a bill in identical form, it goes to the President, who may sign it, let it become law without a signature after ten days, or veto it and return it to Congress with his objections. Congress may then override that veto, but only with a two-thirds vote in both the House and the Senate, a deliberately difficult bar to clear.

📚 PART 2 · HAMILTON'S TWO-PART DEFENSE

Alexander Hamilton defended this “qualified negative,” as it was called at the time, in Federalist No. 73. His argument ran in two parts. First, self-defense: without some check on Congress, a President who displeased the legislature would have no way to protect the office's own constitutional authority from being slowly eroded by statute. Second, and more important for the community as a whole, the veto forces a second, more careful look at legislation passed in haste or under the sway of a passing faction, not a permanent block, since Congress can still override it, but a deliberate pause built into the process.

📚 PART 3 · A LEGISLATIVE POWER LENT TO THE EXECUTIVE
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
The veto is not really an executive power at all in the way appointing ambassadors is. It is a legislative power lent to the executive, proof, as Montesquieu's theory predicted back in Week 4, that pure separation of powers was never really the framers' goal.

What they built instead was a system in which each branch holds a partial check on the others' core function, so that no single branch's mistakes can become law, or stay unlaw, without another branch's participation.
