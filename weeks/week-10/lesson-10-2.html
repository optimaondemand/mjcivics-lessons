📚 PART 1 · THE VETO'S ACTUAL ADDRESS4 min
📖 Glossary of Subject Vocabulary

Reviewing three terms from Lesson 10.1, plus new terms for the veto.

Commander in Chief (kuh-MAN-der in CHEEF)
the President's constitutional role as head of the armed forces.
“high Crimes and Misdemeanors”
the Constitution's phrase for the grounds for impeachment, with no single fixed legal definition.
line-item veto (LYNE-EYE-tem VEE-toh)
the power, held by most state governors but not the President, to strike out specific spending provisions in a budget bill while signing the rest.
pardon (PAR-dun)
the President's power to forgive a federal crime or delay its punishment.
pocket veto (POK-et VEE-toh)
when a President lets a bill die simply by not signing it before Congress adjourns, so it never becomes law.
veto (VEE-toh)
the President's power to reject a bill passed by Congress and return it with objections; Congress can override it with a two-thirds vote in both chambers.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read further: where would you guess the veto power is written in the Constitution, Article I or Article II? (No wrong answer, just check in with yourself.)

It is written into Article I, Section 7, the section describing how a bill becomes law. This is a reminder that the Constitution's separation of powers is not three sealed compartments. It is one continuous process the branches pass back and forth. Once both chambers of Congress pass a bill in identical form, it goes to the President. He may sign it. He may let it become law without his signature after ten days. Or he may veto it and return it to Congress with his objections. Congress may then override that veto, but only with a two-thirds vote in both the House and the Senate, a deliberately difficult bar to clear.

The Constitution's ten-day rule actually creates two different kinds of veto in practice. If Congress remains in session, a President who takes no action at all lets the bill become law automatically after ten days, without his signature. But if Congress adjourns during those ten days, and the President has not signed the bill, it does not become law at all. This second outcome is nicknamed a “pocket veto(POK-et VEE-toh)when a President lets a bill die simply by not signing it before Congress adjourns, so it never becomes law.,” since the President effectively lets the bill die simply by keeping it in his pocket and doing nothing. George Washington, the first President, used the ordinary veto only twice during his entire eight years in office. How often the veto gets used has varied enormously across American history: Franklin Roosevelt vetoed more bills than any other President, 635 in twelve years, while several early presidents, including Thomas Jefferson and John Quincy Adams, never used the veto power at all during their entire time in office.

📚 PART 2 · HAMILTON'S TWO-PART DEFENSE4 min

Alexander Hamilton defended this “qualified negative,” as it was called at the time, in Federalist No. 73. His argument ran in two parts. The first reason was self-defense. Without some check on Congress, a President who displeased the legislature would have no way to protect the office's own constitutional authority from being slowly worn away by ordinary statute. The second reason mattered more for the community as a whole. The veto forces a second, more careful look at legislation passed in haste or under the sway of a passing faction. It is not a permanent block, since Congress can still override it. It is a deliberate pause built into the process. Hamilton called the term “qualified” negative on purpose. A true, unqualified veto, like the one the British King once held over Parliament, would be absolute and final. This one is not. Congress always keeps the last word, if it can find the two-thirds agreement to use it.

📚 PART 3 · A LEGISLATIVE POWER LENT TO THE EXECUTIVE5 min
💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Notice that the veto is not really an executive power at all, in the way appointing ambassadors is. It is a legislative power lent to the executive. This is proof, as Montesquieu's theory predicted back in Week 4, that pure separation of powers was never really the framers' goal.

What they built instead was a system in which each branch holds a partial check on the others' core function. This way, no single branch's mistakes can become law, or stay unlaw, without another branch's participation. Notice, too, that the veto shapes lawmaking even when a President never actually uses it. Congress often adjusts a bill's wording in advance, simply to avoid a veto it expects, which means the mere threat of the qualified negative does real work long before any bill reaches the President's desk.

Overrides are rare in practice, which is itself evidence for Hamilton's design working roughly as intended. Congress has overridden only a small fraction of presidential vetoes across all of American history, since gathering two-thirds of both chambers against a President usually requires a level of bipartisan agreement that is hard to reach except in unusual circumstances. The clearest exception is Andrew Johnson, the same President you read about in Lesson 10.1's account of impeachment. Johnson's relationship with Congress over Reconstruction policy broke down so completely that lawmakers overrode more of his vetoes than any other President's in American history, passing several major Reconstruction laws directly over his objection.

It is worth knowing that the President's veto power is actually weaker in one specific way than the power many state governors hold. Most state constitutions give their governors a line-item veto(LYNE-EYE-tem VEE-toh)the power, held by most state governors but not the President, to strike out specific spending provisions in a budget bill while signing the rest., the power to strike out and reject specific individual spending provisions within a larger budget bill while signing the rest of the bill into law. The President has no such power over federal spending bills. He must sign or veto an entire bill as written, in full, which sometimes forces him to accept spending he dislikes buried inside a bill he otherwise wants, or reject provisions he supports along with ones he opposes.
